Command examples

Try “bend my right elbow” or “turn my left palm down”. You can also bend either knee, straighten a wrist, open a hand, or spread your fingers. To combine movements, type “bend my left elbow then straighten my left elbow”. You can join up to four movements. A new command or a movement key stops the remaining steps.

To see a lab recording, enter “jump forward” and choose a title. For the printer task, enter a hand movement and select Find hand recording. Enter “forecast a reach and its sensory feedback” to see a recorded reach and the nerve signals that followed.

Try “raise my right hand above my shoulder”. The game reports where the virtual wrist finished. NeuroTranslate estimates where it will go next.

To save a movement for replay, close the Command box. Press C to open the recorder. Choose a limb. In Live camera, select Start camera, allow video when asked, then use Record take and Finish take. In Recorded video, play a file and choose Save video take. In Timed pictures, give each picture a time, then choose Track pictures and Save picture take. Press F and type “replay my take”. To save a name, open Named takes, enter a name and choose Save last take.

After saving an arm take, type “forecast my wrists” to estimate the next half second of a wrist path. Choose a lead arm to try the shoulder model. Type “match my arm reach” to compare the path with eight recorded macaque reaches.

    Angles by capture time

    The standalone camera recorder starts its clock when you choose Record take. Movement mirror camera takes and video takes start at the first pose saved. Timed pictures start at the beginning of the saved time interval. Times are rounded down to milliseconds. Half-second rows keep each take’s own times and gaps.

    The range runs from the lowest to the highest visible reading. The frame count includes saved tracking losses. A timeout adds no frame. Movement between readings is unknown.

    Change per second uses consecutive angle readings 50–250 ms apart. A positive rate means the angle grew. Gaps break the sequence. Camera perspective and tracking jitter affect the rates.

    Fingertip distance measurements

    HoloAssist recorded instructors guiding people through everyday tasks. The headset tracked the thumb tip and four fingertips. The two models learned from those optical distances, earlier changes in each gap, the task and instruction duration. Only the word model also uses the recognised categories in the first and second halves of the instruction.

    In an internal check, 26 instructions had usable tracking at both times. Only 15 from three recording groups allowed a same-task comparison with another instruction. For the thumb–index gap, the average error with words was 7.96 mm at half a second and 6.56 mm at one second. Without words it was 7.99 and 6.28 mm. Each group counted equally. The check found no consistent gain from word order.

    These examples use invented hand histories. Four distances can describe many poses. The study did not test whether the person followed the instruction or grasped an object successfully.

    How the forecast works

    In Recent wrist direction, each position gives (outward, up) from that wrist’s own shoulder. One unit equals the height of the video image. The camera already reflects the right arm into the outward direction. Keep the lens facing you.

    The direction estimate starts at the selected frame. It needs three consecutive visible samples spanning at least 90 ms in the past half second. For each axis it takes the median speed between sample pairs at least 30 ms apart. It continues that speed to each horizon. The comparisons use half that speed or hold the wrist still.

    Error is the straight-line distance from the forecast to the later path, in image heights. If the target lies between two visible samples, the table labels the straight-line interpolation. A missing side or a gap over 250 ms breaks the trace and the fit. An interval beyond the last frame has no comparison.

    The shoulder model uses a 30 Hz grid from 14/30 seconds before the cursor through the cursor. It interpolates only between neighboring captures up to 200 ms apart. Both shoulders fix the axes before the forecast. A hidden wrist or elbow blocks that arm. Later measurements use those same axes. Error is the distance between the model and the later wrist in projected shoulder widths.

    Two ridge regressions each learned from 1,024 generated paths. One fit describes the lead arm in those examples and the other describes its partner. Automatic mode gives the two possible assignments equal weight in the same shoulder frame. The left-lead and right-lead settings let you inspect them separately. Handedness and forecast accuracy in people are unmeasured.

    The camera estimates visible movement in a flat image. Hand intention, depth and brain activity are unmeasured. Camera images are neither saved nor uploaded.

    How the paths are compared

    Face the lens. Each visible wrist is measured from its own shoulder in image heights. Outward motion is positive horizontal on either arm, so the right arm is reflected from the raw camera image. Up is positive vertical. The mirrored camera preview is for viewing only.

    The optical hand marker has three study coordinates in shoulder-relative metres. We compare its X and Y to the two camera directions by convention. Camera depth is unknown. Matching keeps direction, subtracts each start and divides both axes by the largest distance from that start. We sample 30 equally spaced instants from start to finish and rank the root-mean-square point distances. Zero means identical normalized paths. The score has no units.

    A tracked take needs five visible samples over at least 200 ms, with no tracking loss between them or gap over 400 ms. The wrist must move more than 2.5% of the image height. Matching excludes leading and trailing time without a visible wrist. Similarity describes the gesture. The electrode counts belong to the selected animal trial.

    Walking measurements used for training

    Adults walked barefoot at a slow metronome pace in a gait laboratory. Skin markers, right-leg muscle voltages and two force plates supplied the training measurements. The fit uses right-foot contacts with verified plate assignment. The game estimates limb angles from pictures. The comparison uses the laboratory's marker positions and muscle voltages.

    On 203 examples from 25 people, with people held out by fold, the mean heel-position error was 1.37 mm and the mean absolute force error was 100 N.     What each input control changes

    The final observed marker position stays fixed. Changing head travel shifts earlier sideways head positions along a straight ramp. Changing scalp amplitude scales the mean and root-mean-square voltage in each of eight time bins. The eye, neck and leg signals stay fixed.

    Both models learn from stored measurements. Their last input falls 125 to just under 129 ms after the rotation starts. They forecast movement 125 ms beyond that point. Movement and muscle activity can affect scalp voltage. These predictions cannot identify the nerve pathways behind a movement.

    Across 880 rotations from 6 people held out of training, the model with scalp voltage missed the sacrum by 7.80 mm and the head by 7.18 mm on average. Without scalp voltage the errors were 7.67 and 7.12 mm. Neither marker showed a reliable improvement from adding scalp voltage over both comparisons.

    How the comparison was made

    The first 362 seconds of the Stanford recording provide 88 complete pairs. The middle second of each cue and following blank was sampled at 1,000 Hz. Each trial’s band-power change is 10 × log₁₀(cue power / blank power), averaged over contacts in the area. Each trial counts once toward the mean for its cue. The middle half spans the 25th to 75th percentiles across these trials.

    Six named areas account for 42 of 46 contacts. Four contacts had no assigned area. No contacts sampled the temporal or occipital cortex. These averages describe the timing of the screen cue. They cannot establish the timing or cause of any individual finger movement.
