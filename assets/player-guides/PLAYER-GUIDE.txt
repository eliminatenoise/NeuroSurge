# Play NeuroSurge

Open https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroSurge/. The page runs locally in your browser after downloading its files. See [preview notes](PREVIEW-NOTES.html) for what is new, the current limitations and credits. For offline local hosting, run `node serve-local.mjs` and open the printed address.

Choose **Play Full** or **Play Lite** on the menu. Wait for the scene to load, then choose how to control the pointer. W A S D move the selected limb, Space grips, E changes the view and R changes the information cards. With a free pointer, drag on an empty part of the scene to look around. Esc shows the instructions and tutorial.

You are slumped back as on a sofa, looking diagonally down your own front. From the bottom of the view up you see your collar bones and both shoulders, your chest and stomach, and your legs running out to your feet. The brain stands ahead of you and above your feet, large and beating slowly. You see it from above, turned to face you: its front is at the bottom and its right half is on your left. The coloured band across it is the motor strip, a simplified map of the body, shaped like a lowercase n: the leg areas at the top in the middle, the arms down each side, the face at the two lower ends. The mirror body sits facing you inside that arch, at your own size, its toes almost touching yours. Think of the strip as a control panel: each coloured area is a button. Looking at a button presses it: the part it names contracts at once, hard, and then goes back to where it was. Each click presses it again. The areas on your left are in cool colours and move your left side; the areas on your right are in warm colours and move your right side. The crossing is the usual one: the areas on your left are in the brain's right half, and each half of the brain mainly moves the opposite side of the body. Two eyeballs hang on their nerves at the brain's front to show which way it faces. At the start they are behind your own body: press E to see them from outside, or lift the brain with Space. One short line at the top gives you one small thing to try at a time.

- **Recorded activity and forecasts** opens the recordings panel. Press **M** for the electrical map. Play or pause one recorded right-wrist movement. Drag the time slider, or use its arrow keys for single samples, Home for the start and End for the last sample. Read the eight scalp sites on a common voltage scale. Choose a scalp or forearm contact to see its trace, or follow both encoder angles in the wrist plot. Open the forecast below the traces and choose one of six times. Compare the measured angles with predictions from wrist motion alone and with forearm or scalp signals added. **Game pathways** lets you choose a region and movement, see its motor and sensory routes, then try the simulated action. Esc returns to the game. In **Hand search**, open the wrist forecast to try an invented movement history or enter your own four earlier positions.
- **F → “show recorded brain activity”** opens a finger-cue recording from one participant. Pick a finger to compare 12–20 Hz and 70–110 Hz power across six sampled areas. Check the spread to see the middle half of trial values. **Recorded activity and forecasts → Recorded cortex** opens the same comparison. The full 3D map opens when you follow its link.
- **F → “forecast my wrists”** estimates where each visible wrist will be 0.267 and 0.500 seconds after a frame you choose. Use the camera recorder below to choose an arm or both arms. Record and finish the take. Move the time slider to compare the forecast with the later camera path. The automatic forecast uses projected shoulder widths. Its axes point toward your right shoulder and toward the top of the picture. The synthetic example works without a camera. On devices with on-device voice, say “predict both wrists”, check the words, then submit.
- **F → “match my arm reach”** compares a finished arm take with eight optical hand-marker recordings from one macaque. Open the camera recorder, choose an arm and record a continuous reach. Choose **Finish take**, press F, then choose **Match my arm reach**. Blue shows the shape of your wrist movement, amber the recorded study hand. Scrub or replay the macaque’s three measured coordinates from the selected trial. The later spike count comes from its cortical electrodes. To explore the recordings with the camera off, choose **Try a recorded example**. [Study measurements and comparison method](scene/src/camera/reach-study/SOURCES.md)
- **F opens the Command box.** Try “Bend my right elbow” and press Enter. Turn either palm up (forearm supination) or down (pronation). Try “turn my left palm down”, “supinate my right forearm” or “right forearm pronation”. The forearm turns briefly, then returns. If the palm already faces the requested direction, it stays there. You can also bend or straighten either knee, wrist or elbow, open or close either hand, and spread the fingers on either side. “Right elbow flexion”, “right elbow extension”, “right hand open” and “right hand close” also work. For a sequence, type “bend my left elbow, then straighten my left elbow”. Join up to four supported movements with “then” and name the side each time. The next step waits for the previous movement to return. Press F again, pause or use manual movement controls to stop the remaining steps. The game uses its built-in movement map. Choose **Use voice** to dictate on a browser that supports on-device recognition, check what it heard, then choose **Move**. Esc or Cancel returns to the game. Type “jump forward” and press Enter. Choose the matching title to see the tracked body markers. Leave the box empty and choose **Find body recording** to see all three titles. “Replay a forward jump” still opens the recording directly. The same three recordings are available in **Recorded activity and forecasts → Whole-body motion**. To search the printer recording with your own words, type a description and choose **Find hand recording**. Select a ranked moment to inspect the tracked hand.
- **C opens camera controls.** In the standalone game it opens the recorder. With the local model connected, it opens the Movement mirror. To use the recorder there, choose **Record a limb take** in the toolbar. In the recorder, select an arm, knee or group under **Follow** before or during capture. Use your own left and right. If you use the keyboard, change the choice with the arrow keys and press **Tab** to reach **Start camera**. Select that button and allow video if the browser asks. Video stays on your device.
- **Choose what the body follows in the Movement mirror.** Use **Body follows** for one limb, a pair or both arms and knees. Use **Forecast only** to move the body with the game controls. **Point to forecast** can be on a different limb. Change the forecast point to start a new window of pictures while the chosen limbs keep following. Pause, seek or finish a clip to release those limbs. A sequence of timed pictures releases them when it ends.
- **Check each selected limb.** **Following** and a green line show a limb the recorder can use. **Show a bend** means it can see the knee and needs a clear starting bend. **Bring into view** means the tracker cannot use that limb from the picture. Keep your shoulders and the selected elbow and wrist or hip, knee and ankle visible. Turn partway sideways for knees. A visible arm or knee can keep moving when another leaves the picture. With the game pointer captured, **1** selects the left arm, **2** the left knee, **3** the right knee and **4** the right arm. Choose **Stop camera** in the recorder or **Stop** in the Movement mirror to finish. [What the camera can follow](scene/src/camera/README.md)
- **Record and replay a camera take.** Choose the limbs you want to follow. When tracking starts, choose **Record take**. For a pair, show each side for at least two frames. **Both arms and knees** can keep a take even if only one limb stays visible for two frames. The four counts show how much of each movement was captured. Choose **Finish take** or let eight seconds elapse. The camera switches off. **Replay take** moves the recorded limbs at the pace of the camera frames. If one side drops out, the other can keep moving. **Dismiss** clears the unnamed take.
  Leave the camera panel open and press **F**. Type **replay my take**, **replay my right arm take** or **replay my take of both knees** and press Enter. **Replay my whole-body take** plays the arms and knees captured in that mode. The camera does not track fingers or spine. You can also dictate those words with **Use voice**, check them and choose **Move**. Typing uses neither camera nor microphone. Opening F stops live tracking or playback and cancels an unfinished recording. To keep this movement for later, open **Named takes** in F, give it a name such as **Warm up**, and choose **Save last take**. After recording another movement, type **replay take named warm up** to play the earlier one. Case and extra spaces do not matter. A partial name leaves the body still. The list shows the exact names, limbs and durations. To see a shorter passage, type **replay 0.5 to 1.5 seconds of take named warm up**. You can also ask for **first 1 second** or **last 1 second** in place of the two times. Times run from the start of the recording. The full take stays available.
- **Compare two saved takes.** In **Named takes**, choose a first and second take and press **Compare takes**. Or type **compare take named warm up with take named second try**. Under **Joint**, pick an angle both takes recorded on the same side. At the elbow or knee, 0° means straight. For arm lift, 0° points down, 90° lies along the shoulder line and 180° points overhead in the camera picture. The left arm uses its saved upper-arm direction. The right uses the avatar’s sideways shoulder target. Each take shows its lowest and highest visible angles, how many saved frames supplied a reading, and when the first and last readings arrived. A gap can hide a larger movement. **Change per second** shows how quickly this angle rose (+) or fell (−) between consecutive readings 50–250 ms apart. Choose **Show intervals** for the start, end and rate of each pair. The covered time adds up just those intervals. Camera position and tracking jitter can change the result.
  To replace a bookmark, save the new take under its existing name and confirm **Replace take**. To remove one, enter its name, choose **Forget by name** and confirm **Forget take**. Named takes survive **Dismiss**. Leaving or reloading the page clears them.
- **Look** by dragging on an empty part of the picture. Look down to see your chest and legs, or press E to look from outside. The muscles that turn your head light as you look. In the eye view, a small look up raises the selected limb at the shoulder or hip. A small look down lowers it. Where its joints end the limb waits, and it joins your look again when you come back. In the view from outside (E) and while Space holds the brain or the mirror body, the look moves no limb.
- **Practise raising or lowering a limb.** After you select a limb, drag up or down on an empty part of the picture. If you chose **Capture mouse**, move the mouse up or down. The game measures from just before the look. The tutorial advances when the virtual wrist or ankle moves at least 2.5 cm relative to its shoulder or hip within 0.35 seconds of game time after the look. Press **E** first if you are looking from outside.
- **Look** at a coloured motor area and rest there for a moment. The movement under your look happens at once: the part is driven hard to its end and rebounds off it, then goes back to where it was and stays there. **Click** to do it again; every click repeats it, also while the part is still on its way back. The area and the muscles it drives light up together while the part contracts and go dark as it goes back: nothing is lit for the way back. A movement that ends where the part already is, such as clenching a closed jaw, strikes its stop: a short twitch. After you try a movement, its area and muscles rest a shade darker, so you can see what is left to try. A cone of light in the same colour runs from that part of the area to each muscle it moves. Your look reaches an area even when your body or the mirror body is in front of it.
- **Try the left wrist.** Look at Left wrist, on the left side of the strip (the brain’s right half). The tip beside the crosshair names the area and the movement under your look. Each area has a part for each of its movements: move your look a little within the area and the tip names another one.
- **Read what you are looking at.** A small tip beside the crosshair names the motor area under it and the one movement your look does there, for example “Left elbow: Bend”. On a body it names the muscle or bone under the crosshair. That tip, the short line at the top, the names on the brain and one small card are the text the game starts with. **R** opens the fuller cards, then hides them all, then shows the small card again.
- **Choose a limb.** Tap or click a limb name at the bottom of the screen while the pointer is free. You can also press **1** for your left arm (the start), **2** for your left leg, **3** for your right leg or **4** for your right arm. The chosen limb itself glows for a moment on your body and on the mirror body. Turn the view yourself if the hand or foot lies outside the picture. **W/A/S/D** then move its hand or foot. A **double click** steps to the next limb, 1, 2, 3, 4 and back again; the second click presses nothing else. Keep the second click held to keep stepping slowly. A click or Space directly on one of your limbs chooses it too.
- **Press and drag one of your limbs.** Press on an arm or a leg and drag: sideways turns it, up bends it and down straightens it, as far as you drag and through the joint’s whole range. The limb glows while you drag it, and it becomes the chosen limb.
- **← and →** put your look on the next area toward that side of the screen. Hold a key to keep stepping. The walk stops at the end of the strip and waits while Space holds something. If a compass arrow crosses the selected area, the arrow fades. Click to use the compass arrow, or Shift-click to press the selected area behind it.
- **Nothing stays moved by the brain.** Every movement from the brain goes back by itself to where the part was before: where you put it with W/A/S/D, a drag or Space, or where it started. **Reset body** on the Esc page puts back what you placed.
- **Head and trunk.** A trunk movement bends the trunk under your head, and a head movement turns the head under your eyes: the mirror body shows the head move and your view stays where your pointer put it. Both go back by themselves.
- **Legs.** A knee bends all the way, and the hip area swings the thigh down and back as well as up, out and in. A leg that swings down can leave your own view; watch it on the mirror body.
- **Click a muscle** on your own body or on the mirror body to switch on the motor area that moves it. The “Looking at” card names the muscle, bone, area or arrow under the crosshair before you click. Bones do nothing.
- **W/A/S/D** move the hand or foot of the chosen limb. **W** reaches it out from the shoulder or hip and **S** draws it back in, along the line between the two as far as the joints allow (where they cannot keep the line, the limb still moves, more slowly and a little curved). **A** and **D** swing the whole limb to your left or right at the shoulder or hip without changing its bend. The limb does not turn inward or outward while you do this; the double tap of Space does that. At the start that is your left hand. Every limb goes through its whole reach whichever way you look: a hand out to a straight arm, back to your chest, across your body and out to the side; a foot out to a straight leg and back until the knee is folded. Your view stays where you put it, even when the hand or foot leaves the picture. The part the keys move glows while you hold them, and the motor area for the joint that moves lights with its muscles. A hand stops at your own trunk. Your limbs pass through the brain and the mirror body. A limb that meets another of your limbs pushes it gently aside.
- **Hold Space** with the crosshair or free pointer on the brain or the mirror body to take hold of it. (Space on one of your own limbs chooses that limb.) While Space is down, both your hands close and the toes of both feet curl, whatever is under the crosshair, and each hand and foot glows as it closes. A click never closes a hand: it only presses. Your hands and toes also close a little as you look down and open as you look up. Then **move the pointer** without pressing a mouse button: your view turns and carries the held thing with it. The mirror body lifts off the ground this way. **W** moves the held thing farther, **S** nearer, and **A/D** sideways. The keys keep the held item at its current height. The hold keeps its thing wherever the crosshair goes. Let go of Space to leave it there.
- **Tap Space** once to take hold and keep holding after the tap. Tap Space again to let go.
- **The mirror body.** It starts as your reflection: your size, level with you, toe to toe. Take it by any part and you move all of it: Space carries it and a drag turns it.
- The game starts with the underside of your limbs showing: palms up, feet turned 45 degrees outward like a V. **Tap Space twice** and all four limbs switch quickly to the overside (palms down, feet turned 45 degrees inward like an A); tap twice again and they switch back. The foot leads the leg's turn, then the shin, then the thigh. The whole arm turns over, the hand first and the upper arm after it, so the front of the arm shows on the underside and its back and outer side on the overside. **Tap twice and hold** and the limbs keep turning slowly between the two sides. Let go and every limb stays where it is. As the underside comes up, the muscles that bend the limbs (the flexors) light for a moment; as the overside comes up, the muscles that straighten them (the extensors) do.
- **Hold Shift** as you click, press Space or drag to reach the other thing on the same line of sight: the muscle in front of a motor area, the brain behind the mirror body, or what is behind an arrow.
- **Turn clockwise or anticlockwise.** Press on the brain or the mirror body and drag round in a big circle: after most of a circle it starts turning the way you are circling, about the line from your eye, and keeps turning as long as you keep circling. Reset brain view puts it back.
- **Light-blue glass.** Whatever is hidden behind something shows through it as a light-blue outline: a hand under your body, the mirror body behind your leg, the motor strip behind your knee, a body behind the brain. The crosshair still takes what is in front, and Shift reaches behind; only a limb that is almost wholly hidden behind its own body, drawn as filled glass, is taken directly.
- **Press and drag** on the brain or the mirror body to turn it. Drag the mirror body sideways to turn it round, all the way if you like, and up or down to tilt it, until you see it from straight above or below. While Space holds either object, hold the mouse button and drag to turn it. W/A/S/D keep carrying it. On your own limbs, drag sideways to turn, up to bend and down to straighten. Where you press decides which joint bends: on the left upper arm, up and down raise and lower the arm at the shoulder; on the left forearm or hand they bring the hand nearer or farther.
- **Click an arrow** for a small step, or hold it to keep going. The grey arrows turn the brain in place: each brings round the side it stands on, so the upper one shows more of the brain's back and the lower one more of its front. The green arrows turn the mirror body round (left and right) or tilt it (the top one shows it more from above, the bottom one more from below). In a wide window, look to either side above its head for the green up arrow. Press **E** to see the arrows from behind. Green still turns the mirror body and grey still turns the brain. A turned or tilted mirror body stands a little farther back where it needs the room. Reset brain view on the Esc page stands it upright again, facing you. If you captured the mouse, press Esc and choose Resume to click the arrows with a free pointer.
- **E** switches to an outside view from behind your head, and back. If Space is holding the brain or mirror body, E lets go before changing the view.
- **R** shows more information. The game starts with a short reading card and the tip. Press R for More detail and the colour key. Press R again to hide the cards, or once more to bring back the short card.
- **Esc** pauses and opens the controls and settings. Click **Resume** or press **Esc** again to keep the pointer free. Choose **Capture mouse** to turn the view with each mouse movement. If capture is unavailable, drag on empty space. **Reset body** puts every body part back where it started. **Reset brain view** puts the brain and the mirror body back. The settings there change the look speed, whether up and down are flipped, the picture quality and the lifted outer muscles, and hide or restart the “Try this” guide. Thin outlines between limbs are drawn at every picture quality; the soft shading in the creases between muscles is drawn only at full quality.

The six right-arm command names come from Ofner and colleagues’ experiment. Participants followed visual cues with the right arm supported. Here you supply the words and the avatar briefly moves. The left-side commands use the same game mechanics. The game’s fixed forearm axis approximates the moving axis measured in people. [Original movement cues, Fig. 1](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0182578) · [Forearm rotation measured in vivo](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20696507/)

The avatar’s coloured brain and muscle signals are simulated and unitless. A clicked muscle lights a whole motor area, not a spot for that one muscle. A movement that contracts and then goes back is a rule of this game. Movement limits, physiological mappings and movement mechanics are simplified and still need checking.

Open **Recorded activity and forecasts → Shared model** and choose what to forecast. For a distance in centimetres, try a wrist or rat movement. Select **Recorded maze trip** under rat displacement to reveal the camera measurement. For an angle in degrees, choose **Right-wrist angles**. Read across each row for extension and radial deviation. The first table compares three forecasts as you add forearm muscle activity and then scalp voltage to the wrist angles.

The second table starts both corrections from the same movement-only forecast. One adjusts it using the earlier angles. The other also uses four forearm signals. Choose **Invented forearm example** to compare them on the same half-second of input. The examples contain no measured future angle. **Save offline copy** keeps a working viewer you can open without an internet connection. **Enter my own measurements** lets you try your own history locally. Press **Esc** or choose **Back to game** to resume.

### Forecast heel movement and plate force

Press **F**, enter **forecast heel movement** and press Enter. You can also say **show the walking forecast** with **Use voice**, check the words and press Enter. **Recorded activity and forecasts → Walking forecast** opens the same model.

The example supplies invented heel and forefoot marker positions and muscle voltages. The slider changes the distance both markers travelled along laboratory X while keeping their final positions fixed. The model predicts the signed change in heel position in millimetres and the force on the right plate in newtons, 100 ms later. Press **Esc** to resume the avatar. [Source and measurement details](scene/src/activity/gait-pair/SOURCES.md)

### Forecast muscle and movement

Press **F**, type **forecast muscle and movement** and submit. Add **while walking** or **while standing** to choose the starting posture. With **Use voice**, check the words before submitting. You can also choose **Recorded activity and forecasts → Muscle and movement**, using the on-screen Command box or Recorded activity and forecasts buttons.

One model predicts two things after the scene tilts. The first table shows the electrical activity over four muscles on each leg, averaged as root-mean-square voltage over the next eighth of a second. The plot shows where a marker on the sacrum would move during that same time. All 32 predicted positions are listed below the plot, in millimetres from the last observation.

Try another posture or rotation while keeping the same invented history. Change **Past leg voltage** to raise or lower the amplitude over all eight muscles. The forecast updates as you change each input. The experiment recorded electrodes and a marker. Nothing was measured for the game's invented event. [Experiment and model](scene/src/activity/muscle-motion/SOURCES.md)

### Forecast movement after a visual rotation

Press **F**, enter **forecast my balance**, then press Enter. With **Use voice**, read the transcript before pressing Enter. You can also choose **Recorded activity and forecasts → Visual balance**. The toolbar has both controls.

In the original experiment people walked while the scene in their headset tilted. Here the walk and its electrical signals are invented. Switch the tilt direction, edit the head’s sideways travel, or change the amplitude of the scalp voltages. The two forecasts show how far the head and sacrum markers move along each recorded axis in the next 125 ms. The second model learned without scalp voltage. Both include eye and muscle signals. There is no measured outcome for this example. [Source and model details](scene/src/activity/visual-balance/SOURCES.md)

### Compare two walking instructions

Press **F**, type **forecast my usual walk** or **forecast fast walking without running**, then press Enter. “Forecast as usual” and “forecast fast but not running” take you directly to the study’s two phrases. With **Use voice**, check the transcript before pressing Enter. You can also open **Recorded activity and forecasts → Walking instructions**.

Switch the instruction to see the predicted pelvis and right heel displacements 100 ms later. Both forecasts start from the same 21 invented positions per landmark. The second column of numbers comes from a separate fit that saw only marker history and time since right foot strike. Read each row in millimetres along the forward, left or vertical axis. The simulation stays paused until you return to the game. [Measurement details and credits](scene/src/activity/walking-instruction/SOURCES.md)

### Generate a walk or a run

Press **F**, type **generate walk** or **generate run**, and press Enter. The game pauses while the model draws its predicted stride as 18 moving points. “Generate walking”, “generate a run” and “generate jogging” also work. Dictation fills the same box. Press Enter after checking the words.

Use **Pause** to stop, **Replay** to watch again, or the time slider to inspect any of the 25 samples. **Rotate view** turns the points in 3D. Open **Inspect a body point** for one landmark’s path and its three coordinates. **Back to game** or Escape returns to the same game. With reduced motion enabled, choose **Play** to start.

These two actions come from a model fitted to optical movement recordings. Each word produces the same 0.60-second trajectory each time. The game’s joint commands move its avatar. **Find body recording** plays measurements from a named trial. Arbitrary action generation and hardware control remain outside this preview. [Model, source data and permissions](scene/src/word-motion/README.md)

## Compare wrist and pelvis forecasts

Press **F**, enter **forecast my wrist lift** or **forecast my pelvis path**, and press Enter. With on-device dictation, check the words before choosing **Move**. You can also open **Recorded activity and forecasts → Wrist and pelvis** and pick a movement task.

Change **Earlier muscle amplitude** or **Earlier marker travel** to edit the invented history. In the pelvis study you can also select standing or walking and pick the direction the headset scene turned. The two result columns show what a model trained on both studies and a model trained on this task alone predict from exactly those inputs. The three graphs trace future marker displacement. Expand **Read the 32 positions from each model** for every coordinate in millimetres.

The wrist study keeps muscle amplitudes in the authors’ recording units. The pelvis study uses microvolts. A missing channel reads **Unavailable**. The two examples are separate experiments and have no measured future. Esc returns you to play.

In **Recorded activity and forecasts → Wrist and pelvis**, choose **Body-part maps vs. early/late maps** to try two equally sized movement models. One model uses a different output map for each task. The other blends its two maps as time advances. Both see the same invented history. Adjust earlier travel or muscle amplitude to see how each forecast responds. Each table uses the selected study’s units. [Compare the models against the recorded movements](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/Interoceptomics/cross-limb-movement/)

In **F**, type **forecast my wrist lift with scalp** or **forecast my pelvis path with scalp**. You can also choose **With vs. without scalp** in **Recorded activity and forecasts → Wrist and pelvis**. Change the size of the invented scalp signal while keeping the electrode selection fixed. Both models still see the same muscle activity and movement. Only one reads the changing scalp signal. You can also change which sites are available, which changes the input mask for both models. [Open a measured scalp and movement recording](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/Interoceptomics/cross-limb-movement/)

In **F**, type **replay a muscle stretch** or **forecast sensory spikes**. You can also open **Recorded activity and forecasts → Stretch and spikes**. Follow one rat muscle-stretch recording from the original archive. Each step compares the expected number of sensory nerve impulses with the count actually recorded in the next 20 ms. The input menu switches between earlier firing, mechanics, and both together. The example runs locally with learned weights. It does not move the avatar, read a device, or control a body. [Explore the interactive recording](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/NeuroTranslation/muscle-spindle/)

Press **F** and type **move the cylinder to target 3 with the left hand**. Choose any numbered target from 1 through 9. **Recorded activity and forecasts → Move a cylinder** opens the same forecast. Try a different destination while keeping the first 200 milliseconds of the recording fixed. The recorded outcome appears only when the requested destination matches the original trial.

Press **F** and type **forecast brain activity after a push**, **forecast brain activity after a reach cue**, or **forecast brain activity during an unbumped reach**. Each opens **Recorded activity and forecasts → Next-day brain activity** at that recorded event. Select an electrode and a time to compare the count measured over 100 milliseconds with two forecasts made from earlier signals.

Press **F** and type **replay a perturbed reach** or **forecast a cued reach**. Both open **Recorded activity and forecasts → Cortical reach**, with a different part of the same recorded trial selected. Move through the four forecast times to compare the measured 100-ms handle displacement with the fitted models’ predictions. One model reads the previous handle trajectory and available cues. The other also reads counts from 30 sorted cortical units. These actions inspect archived measurements; they do not start the camera or move the avatar. [Measured arm and cortical activity](https://eliminatenoise.github.io/Interoceptomics/cortical-reach/)

## Preview a grip from words

Press **F**, type **preview grip: open the cover** and press Enter. Or type an instruction and choose **Preview grip**. If on-device dictation is available, **Use voice** fills the same field. Check the words before opening the preview.

Read the four rows as distances from the right thumb tip. A prediction of −2 mm means the two tips move 2 mm closer than their starting gap. Try “close the cover” with the same fictional hand. Switch to **Hand and task only** to see what the comparison model predicts when it has the same history, task and assumed speaking time. Neither model sees your hand.

In **F → Forecast my wrists**, use a finished **C** arm take or choose **Try a synthetic example**. The automatic forecast needs 0.467 seconds of ordinary movement with both shoulders in view. Each wrist also needs its own visible elbow. When one arm drops out, the other can still have a forecast. Move the time slider to compare a prediction with the later path.

The model learned two possible arm roles from synthetic movements. It averages their predictions in the same shoulder frame, giving each role equal weight. You can inspect the left-lead and right-lead forecasts separately. The diagram keeps its axes fixed for both horizons. A camera take uses the original frame times, with interpolation onto a 30 Hz grid. Forecast accuracy on people is untested.

**Recent wrist direction** estimates movement from wrist offsets in image heights. The game uses it automatically for older takes that have only offsets.

## Movement mirror with the local NeuroTranslate checkpoint

Place the extracted `neurotranslate-0.0` model folder beside the game folder. Open **Play movement mirror** for your operating system. Python 3.10 or later and NumPy are required. To use an existing installation, run `python play-joint.py --model-dir "/path/to/neurotranslate-0.0"`. The address it prints opens the game menu. Choose **Play Lite** in the offline ZIP, or **Play Full** or **Play Lite** from the source folder. Press **C** while playing to open the Movement mirror.

- **C · Follow movement** opens the source controls in the 3D game. Use **Body follows** for the limbs to move and **Point to forecast** for a point such as the left knee. Choose **Start camera**, then keep those joints in view. **Stop** or **Close** releases the camera. A local video uses its decoded frame clock. For still images, supply their times in seconds, or name them with their capture times, such as `0.10.jpg`. A rewind starts a new history.
- **F · Command box** accepts requests such as “Lift my left wrist above my shoulder.” On-device dictation, where the browser supports it, fills the same text field. Confirm the wording to run it. The two-link simulator checks wrist height against the shoulder and reports clearance and shortfall in its 640 × 480 drawing plane. The full 3D game checks its drawn wrist against its actual shoulder in world metres. It aims for 5 cm clearance with 0.5 cm tolerance and reports any shortfall. The forecast uses five projected samples of that drawn rig.
- Green points come from the virtual arm or the camera tracker. The amber ring shows where the checkpoint forecasts the selected point will be 120 ms after the last of five input images. The readout compares it with the same point in the closest visible image within 40 ms of the target time. If the point is hidden or the images are too far apart, the model waits for a new sequence. The tracker estimates positions in two dimensions. The footage stays in the browser.
- **Fingertip touch** is available with the Touch study checkpoint. Open **Play fingertip touch** for your operating system, or run `python play-joint.py --touch` from the game folder. Open the Command box and ask “How many spikes will the touch nerve send?” Set the distance the virtual plate slides and choose an invented earlier spike history. The result is an expected count 20–40 ms ahead. The spring supplies the force for this example.
- **Virtual brain** ranks three invented unit histories with the same checkpoint. The probabilities apply to the next occupied bin with exactly one spike. The game’s anatomical brain colours remain simulated. Neither camera nor speech supplies neural measurements.

The checkpoint digest appears after a successful forecast. The camera, timestamped stills, archive playback and virtual arm share the landmark-motion head. The virtual spike example uses the rat unit-ranking head. For human camera use, this experimental model transfers from tracked animal movement. It predicts two-dimensional image positions in pixels. No physical-device accuracy figure has been established.
