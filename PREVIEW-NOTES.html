Play NeuroSurge · Source code · Desktop launchers

Make the connection visible.

Look at a motor area on the brain and the part it names moves at once. Click to do it again. Click a muscle and its motor area lights up, in the same colour. NeuroSurge turns that two-way link into a browser game: a skinless body you look out of, a glass brain you can carry and turn, and a mirror body that copies everything you do. Your computer runs the 3D scene and the simulation; the host only supplies the files.

The Body update, prepared 11 November 2026, revised four times on 12 November, three times on 17 November and six times on 18 November. It replaces the interaction update of 10 November and has not yet had an independent browser review.

Third revision of 17 November: lying back with the whole body in view, and muscles that stay on their bones

  • Lying back as on a declined sofa. The body used to sit upright, so from the eyes only the hands and feet showed in a level look. It now lies back (no sofa is drawn): hips straight, legs level, the back rounded forward 68 degrees and the neck 22, which brings the head upright. The game starts with the look 20 degrees below level and the brain 20 degrees lower than before. The first view holds the whole motor strip, the mirror body under it, and your own feet, legs, pelvis, both arms and chest, all at once. The mirror body lies back too, since it is your reflection.
  • Muscles stay on their bones. A muscle that crosses a joint was drawn as one rigid piece fitted between its bones, so at a bent knee, a lifted thigh, a raised arm or a bent trunk its ends stood off the bones. Every such muscle (226 of 232) is now bent with its joint: each of its vertices follows the two bones nearest to it. Checked in pictures for a bent knee, a lifted thigh, a raised right arm and a bent right elbow, alone and together.
  • Number keys with the cards off. The number keys now do the movements listed in the tip beside the crosshair, at once, and keep working for the last area shown while you look down at your body. In the two uploads earlier that day they did nothing unless the cards were on.

Sixth revision of 18 November: a flatter view, a new colour code with links, looking arms and pressing does

  • The view from your eyes is taken through a longer lens from further back: depth is flattened, so your legs and arms are about twice as large in the picture and far things look nearer. The whole torso is in the first picture. The camera is not at the eyes.
  • The motor strip has a new colour code: the brain’s right half, which moves your left side, is in cool colours and its left half in warm ones; each body part has its own family. Looking at an area draws a line in its colour to the part it moves, crossing the midline; the crosshair on a muscle shows the area that moves it.
  • Looking at an area now only arms it. A click or a number key does the movement. Holding a number key moves the limb part-way.
  • An area that switches on blinks like a neon tube on the strip, on the muscles of both bodies and along its line, and the active tissue shudders. With reduced motion the glow is steady. It is a picture of a simulated signal.
  • The layered stack of muscles (outer ones floating slightly over deep ones) is drawn again; it had been off without notice since 17 November. Shading is smoother close up and joint surfaces carry cartilage.
  • Not right yet: looking around is slower than before; the first prompt and the Esc page still describe looking as pressing; the mirror body is smaller than a true reflection and stands lower; muscles of the shoulder girdle and back are still torn in the start posture. These packages were published before their independent checks.

Fifth revision of 18 November: bulk, vessels, the arm turned over

  • Both bodies have bulkier limb muscles, wider forearms, wrists, hands, shins, ankles and feet (bones included), a thicker neck and a more marked abdomen. Every finger and toe has a rounded sleeve of soft tissue with a blunt tip. These are deliberate changes away from the source body; the amounts are in DESIGN-DECISIONS.md section 97.
  • The brain is a pale pearl-rose, plainly different from the red-brown muscles. Its arteries are the anatomy source’s own meshes; its veins and sinuses were laid by hand along textbook courses and are a typical arrangement, not this brain’s own. The main vessels of both legs and the right arm lie on both bodies.
  • The limb switch turns the whole arm over: the front of the arm shows on the underside, its back and outer side on the overside.
  • A muscle that is active and shortening fills out at its belly. This was not seen working in the game itself.
  • Not right yet: the hand can swing into the thigh or chest on the overside (nothing stops limbs yet); the fingers read as white sausages; the arteries are a strong red; on the legs the thigh shows the same muscles on both sides of the switch. These three packages were published before their independent checks.

Fourth revision of 18 November: painted bodies, a room, joints

  • A fault repaired. In the three revisions before this one, every muscle was drawn as one rigid piece: the skinning that lets a muscle bend with its joints had stopped running when the spine was rebuilt, and nothing said so. It runs again. While things move the game is about a fifth slower than those revisions, which skinned nothing.
  • The bodies are painted: smooth light-to-deep shading with a warm shadow side, fibre bundles along each muscle with wet highlights, thin even contours, a clean lower belly.
  • Everything hangs in a dark operating theatre seen out of focus, in place of the black void: a tiled floor far below with a pool of light, a curtain wall for a horizon, lamp heads overhead, a few motes of dust.
  • The limb switch turns the feet 45 degrees outward, like a V (the underside, where the game starts), and 45 degrees inward, like an A, led by the foot, then the shin, then the thigh.
  • The kneecap stays on the bending knee. Joint angles are limited in combination, so stacked movements stop at positions a body reaches; “Bend knee” from the start posture now ends at 120 degrees. Small coupled movements were added at the knee, ankle, wrist and hip.
  • Not right yet: with the right arm fully raised the armpit is drawn as flat sheets and the shoulder’s top narrows to a point; fibres follow each mesh’s long line, not always the anatomy; from outside (E) the brain is so large that the body is a small figure on it.

Second revision of 18 November: slumped back, the whole torso in view, the strip under the ankles

  • You start slumped back as on a sofa: the pelvis rolled under, the upper back straight and leaning back, the neck bent, the legs level and the limbs stretched out with soft elbows and knees. In a third revision of the same day the legs lie 7 degrees apart each instead of 12, so the ankles are closer together; the feet still clear each other on the overside.
  • The first view holds your whole torso at once: the collar bones along the lower edge, both shoulders in the corners, a flat chest, the stomach, the pelvis, and both legs out to the feet. For that the view is taken from 12 cm above the eyes with a field of view of 116 degrees, and the chest muscles are drawn at about two thirds of the source model’s depth.
  • The brain lies beyond and below your feet and is larger; the motor strip is about 800 px wide on a 1600 px picture. Its middle shows between your legs and the crosshair starts on it. Your legs hide part of it: the crosshair still takes an area behind a leg.
  • The game starts with the underside of your limbs showing (palms up). A double tap of Space switches all four limbs quickly to the overside and back; a double tap held turns them slowly between the two.
  • Not right yet: the stomach is a narrow band; the brain is not tilted to face you, and from outside (E) it hides your pelvis and legs from most sides; the left arm reaches level into the picture instead of lying along the thigh. This revision was published before its independent check had finished.

Revision of 18 November: a brain that beats, a back that bends at every vertebra

  • The brain beats about once a second on a slow swell, with fine vessels on its surface. An area that is switched on makes the tissue round its key swell and flush in the key’s colour, with one ring running outward; the beat quickens a little while areas are on. Carried or turned, the tissue trails and settles like firm jelly. With reduced motion set, none of this moves. It is a picture of a simulated signal, not a measurement.
  • The back bends at every vertebra, the ribs go with their vertebrae, and the shoulders roll forward with the bend. The game starts with the look 36 degrees below level; your belly, pelvis, legs and feet are in view above your chest, with both forearms on your thighs.
  • The brain is drawn about half as wide at the start so that the whole strip fits above the mirror body. The view from outside (E) keeps the whole body in the picture.
  • Not done yet, and asked for: both shoulders in the start picture, a flat chest in place of the two mounds, and the crosshair starting on the motor strip. Three builders could not get them with the camera at the eye; the next revision sets the camera back and thins the chest muscle.

Second revision of 17 November: hold and move, a living brain, a look all the way round

  • Hold with Space, move with the pointer. While Space holds the brain, the mirror body or one of your limbs, the pointer now moves that thing across the view, left, right, up and down, and the view stays where it is. Before, the look carried the thing and nothing seemed to move on screen, and the mirror body could not leave the ground. W, A, S and D still move it sideways, farther and nearer. A hold or a drag keeps the thing it started on, wherever the crosshair goes.
  • The whole mirror body. Since 19 November any part of the mirror body is the mirror body: Space on it carries all of it and a drag turns all of it. Its arms and legs no longer move your own.
  • Both hands close. While Space holds anything, the right hand closes like the left, with its muscles and its part of the strip lit.
  • Moving lights what moves. As you look around, the neck muscles that turn or nod your head light, and the trunk’s when the trunk turns or bends; the left shoulder’s light as that arm follows your look; a limb you hold lights the muscles of each joint that moves. The glow follows the speed and fades when you stop.
  • A living brain with keys. The brain is drawn as tissue: pink-grey cortex with lit folds, dark sulci and a wet sheen, in place of dark glass. Each motor area is a separate raised key in a strong colour of its body part’s family, and no two neighbours look alike. The key under the crosshair lights inside a white frame; an activation pushes it down for a moment; while an area is on, it alone shines with a halo, a slow pulse and a running ring, and the other keys dim.
  • A look all the way round. The look reaches 180 degrees to either side, 88 down and 85 up. The neck and chest turn as far as before; past their end your eyes turn the rest of the way. You can look over your shoulder at your own shoulder and back.
  • Smaller things. The Esc page’s short form has no paragraphs. The brain in the loading picture beats.

Revision of 17 November: no start page, a quiet screen, a wider view

  • No start page and no loading page. The game used to open on a page of instructions with a “Start now” button while its files loaded. Now you watch the parts come together instead: each bone and muscle flies into place on a standing body as its file arrives, and the brain is assembled above it, tipped toward you with the motor strip lit. A thin bar shows how far the loading is. The picture keeps moving while the page builds the body, and fades out over the game’s first view; the game then begins at once. The picture is drawn from the same files the game loads, in the atlas’s standing posture and in plain colours; it checks nothing, and the game still verifies every file itself.
  • The main controls for five seconds. For the first five seconds the main controls are shown either side of that picture: W A S D move, Space grabs, E changes the view, R shows information; on the trackpad or mouse, moving looks, a click presses and a drag turns. Then they fade. Esc still shows the full instructions.
  • A quiet screen. The game starts with the cards of text off. A small tip beside the crosshair names the motor area under it and lists its movements with their number keys, like the legend on a button; on a body it names the muscle or bone. One short line at the top says what to try next. The names on the brain have one line each, and one small line at the bottom left says the activity is simulated. R brings the side cards back.
  • A wider view and a quicker look. The view from your eyes is 108 degrees from top to bottom, about 136 across a 16:9 window (it was 90 and 121). At the start both arms and your legs down to the knees are in view; looking down shows your chest, both arms and your legs together. The look turns half as far again for the same pointer travel, so the ends of the head’s turn can be reached on a trackpad. Things close to the edge of so wide a view are drawn stretched.
  • The Space double tap turns the wrists and ankles. The limb turn used to be led by the shoulders and hips through their whole ranges. Now both forearms turn the palms up or down, so the front and then the back of the forearm and hand are shown, and both ankles turn the soles outward or inward. The upper arms and thighs only follow, by 14 and 10 degrees for a whole stroke.

Fourth revision of 12 November: the mirror body turns all the way round, a whole left shoulder, no white square

  • Turn the mirror body all the way round, and tilt it. The side arrows by the mirror body, or a sideways drag on it, now turn it through a full circle either way, 15 degrees a click, so you can see its back. Before, a turn stopped part of the way: near 171 degrees one way and 123 the other. The top and bottom arrows, and a drag up or down, now tilt it instead of sliding it away and back: the top arrow shows it more from above, the bottom one more from below, as far as the view from straight above or straight below. While it is turned or tilted the mirror body stands back along the ground where it needs the room, by up to about 45 cm, and comes forward again as you turn it back. After a full turn, or a tilt and back, it is the exact reflection again. A tilt lifts its feet off the ground; at the start and after Reset brain view it is upright on the ground, facing you.
  • The left shoulder stays whole. When you looked far to the left, the muscles round your left shoulder swung away from the trunk with the upper arm and left an open end; at a look to the left and down they showed as a pointed fin beside the view. Those muscles now stay with the chest at their upper end, follow the upper arm at their lower end and are spread round the joint in between. The mirror body shows the same shoulder.
  • No white square. At one exact look, straight ahead and about 8 degrees down, a pure-white square about 280 pixels across sat a little right of the middle of a 1920 by 1080 view. One pixel of the glass brain’s see-through layer came out as a value that is not a number, and the glow step spread it into the square. The glass and glow terms can no longer give such a value, and the glow step now ignores one if it meets it. The fault was also in the third revision as uploaded.

Third revision of 12 November: the head and the trunk, and a card that fits

  • A trunk movement keeps your view. Resting your look on “Belly and back” used to lean the trunk back and throw the view up, off the area you had just chosen. A held trunk movement now bends the trunk under your head: the neck takes up the difference, and your view stays where your look put it. While you look up at the brain, a forward bend is drawn smaller than the 80 degrees it holds, about 40 degrees with the look at the height of the trunk area and 60 with a level look. It shows whole when you look down.
  • A head movement says how to bring your head back. A head movement still moves your view, because you see from your head. For five seconds the card at the bottom left now says so, and how to undo it: “Your head moved, so your view moved with it. Press 1 to bring your head back.” The number is the key of the opposite movement on the information card. A head turn has no opposite on the same card: in this game the neck area on the other half of the brain turns it back, and the line says that instead.
  • A press that only brings a part back says so. A movement switched on while its part is held the other way first brings the part back to where it started; the movement itself takes one more press. The numbered list now marks such a movement “first press brings it back”, and after the key the card at the bottom left says: “Key 1 brought the part back to where it started. Press 1 again for the movement itself.” A key that moves nothing says that the movement is already there.
  • The short card shows each part whole or leaves it out. When the short information card ran out of room, its last rows were cut through the letters. It now leaves out whole parts, in this order: the lead sentence, the signal path, the muscles that work the other way, the muscles that pull. The title, the strip part, the signal bar and the numbered movements always stay, and R opens the card with every part.
  • The card follows the movement. When resting your look on an area moves the body part, the information card takes that area in the same frame. Before, it took the area one frame later. After a head movement, which moves the view, the card could then stay on the area before, with the number keys still acting there.

Second revision of 12 November: easier to follow, and a learning tool

  • The brain above, the mirror body below. The glass brain now sits in the upper part of the view and the mirror body below it. The body no longer stands in front of the middle of the motor strip, where the leg and foot areas are. To fit, the strip is a little smaller: about 42 % of the width in a 1920 by 1080 window. The brain’s bottom arrow sits a little higher than the middle of its frame’s lower side, in the hollow of the strip, to keep clear of the mirror body’s top arrow.
  • Numbered movements and number keys. The information card lists the movements of the motor area you look at, or looked at last, with numbers. Keys 1 to 9 do the movement with that number, the same way a click on one of its muscles does. The list stays while you look down at the body, so you can look at an area, look at the body, press a number and watch the part move. A movement that would not move the body from where it is now is marked “already there”.
  • Arrow keys step along the strip. ← and → put your look on the next motor area toward that side of the screen, in the order of the body along the strip, so finding a small area no longer needs a steady hand. Held keys keep stepping. Areas hidden in the current view are passed over, and a line says when there are no more that way.
  • The card waits for your look to rest. The motor areas are small and lie side by side. The information card now takes a new area only after your look has rested on it for about a third of a second, so the areas you cross on the way to the body no longer change the card or the area the number keys act on. The “Looking at” card and the glow on the brain still answer at once.
  • Names you can learn from. The “Looking at” card names the motor area and then its movement, for example “Left knee · Bend left knee”. The information card names the part of the strip and the half of the brain the area is drawn on, for example “Left-leg motor area (brain’s right half)”. That is where the area is drawn; it is not a claim that only that half of the brain drives the part.
  • The guide starts with the link. The “Try this” line now begins with what the game teaches: look at a left-arm area, press a number from the card, look at another body part’s area, then look down at the body and press a number to watch the leg. The hand, Space and arrow goals come after. There are 12 goals; progress saved by the older guide still loads.
  • Legs reach every hip and knee movement. A knee now bends all the way with the thigh level, and the hip area swings the thigh down and back, to 20 degrees behind the line of the trunk, as well as up, out and in. A thigh lifted past 100 degrees is drawn with the knee giving way, as a sitting person’s knee does. The invisible ground is only where the two bodies sit at the start: a leg that is moved or held has no floor under it.
  • A shorter Esc page that says what the strip is. The Esc page opens with the short instructions, and “Show the full instructions” has the rest. The steps now say that the coloured band is the motor strip, drawn as a simplified map of the body, that each half of the brain mainly moves the opposite side, and in which order the body lies along the strip.

First revision of 12 November

  • Space is the one hand key. Hold Space to take hold of what you look at. Tap it to take hold and keep holding, and tap again to let go. Tap twice and all four limbs turn once; tap twice and hold and they keep turning, slowly. The F key is no longer used. While a hold lasts, your left hand closes, the hand part of the left-arm strip lights and the crosshair shows a ring. The closed hand is a sign of the hold only: its fingers do not touch what is carried.
  • Each activation shows what belongs together. W, A, S and D light only the shoulder part of the left-arm strip, with the elbow part for W and S, and the muscles that make that movement, instead of the whole arm region. While an area is lit, one line in its colour joins it to the body part it moves on the mirror body. The information card names the key, the strip part, the movement and the muscles. The muscles listed for each direction are the usual textbook movers, lit at a made-up level.
  • R reads the information. R replaces I. The side cards are on at the start. R adds More detail and the colour key, R again hides all of it, and R again brings the cards back. The status card is shorter than before.
  • The brain takes about half the width. The motor strip spans about half the width of the view instead of all of it. The second revision moved the brain above the mirror body and made the strip a little smaller again (see above).
  • A neon flash instead of a power-up. An area and its muscles come on at once and flash once, like a neon tube being switched on, then glow steadily. The glow answers within a twentieth of a second.
  • Gloss, and lighter ink. Muscles, tendons, bones and motor areas were matte and now carry a glossy highlight. The comic ink is toned down: thinner, lighter outlines and much less hatching and stipple.
  • Slightly bent limbs. A stretched right elbow keeps about 12 degrees of bend and each knee about 10. Only the drawing changes: the joint angles and limits still count from straight. Since the second revision no ground holds the heels, so the slight knee bend lowers each heel a few centimetres below the level you sit on. The left arm already kept a small bend at full reach.
  • The mirror body keeps its distance in the view over your shoulder. E moves the camera 1.4 m behind you. The mirror body now slides away by the same distance and comes back when you press E again.
  • Faster, with the same picture. Holding and carrying the brain or the mirror body, the pose of the body, the skinning of the left arm and the drawing of the brain’s glass take less work in each frame and give the same picture. With Picture quality: Auto, the game now switches to a cheaper picture only when that makes the frames faster. Frame rates are under Known limitations.

From 11 November

  • Through your own eyes. The picture is taken from between the eyes of the body, and the eyes stay fixed in the head. Moving the mouse turns your head and neck; past their reach your chest turns and your trunk bends, with your legs left on the ground. Your own body is always solid: nothing on it fades or turns see-through when the glass brain is near. Your head and face are behind the picture, so you see them on the mirror body or from behind.
  • A wide view, not an extreme one. The view is 90 degrees from top to bottom, about 121 degrees across a 16:9 window. That is at the top of ordinary game settings: wide enough for the brain and both bodies, without the strong stretching near the edges of a wider view.
  • Sitting with legs and arms forward. You sit upright on an invisible flat ground, with your legs out in front, side by side and about a hip width apart, and both arms reaching forward at shoulder height. No chair or floor is drawn, and the ground is only where you sit at the start: since the second revision of 12 November a leg that moves can swing below it. A stretched elbow or knee keeps a slight bend. The left arm is the game arm: its hand keeps a fixed place in your view, so its elbow bends or straightens as you look around.
  • A true mirror body. At the start, and after Reset brain view, the mirror body is the exact reflection of your body in an upright mirror a few centimetres beyond your farthest point: the same size, the same height, on the same ground, upright. Your left arm shows on the same side of the screen, as in a real mirror. Like a real reflection seen from your eyes, its head is at eye level, so it does not fill the height of the view.
  • A motor strip across a little under half the view. At the start the whole brain is brought nearer and scaled so that the motor strip spans a little under half the width of the view (about 42 % in a 1920 by 1080 window). The strip is never moved on the brain: it is the atlas's own precentral gyrus, with the hand areas on the hand knob and the face areas running in order down the side of the brain. Each half now has 27 areas, 54 in all, with an area for each finger, the nose and the throat. Area borders are smooth ink lines, and the labels come in matching pairs on both halves. The strip is drawn in the same comic style as the bodies: light bands across the gyrus, darker banks where it dives into the sulci on either side, an ink line along each sulcus and hatching in the shadows. The fold shading is worked out from the atlas's own brain meshes, a display choice; no folds are invented. The glass brain has inked contours.
  • One colour per area, the same on all three. Every motor area has its own colour, a light or dark shade of its body part's colour family: blues for the left arm, oranges for the right arm, purples for the left leg, yellows for the right leg, greens for belly and back, pinks for face and neck, and grey for the in-between areas, which move nothing. When an area switches on, it and the muscles it drives glow in that colour on your body and on the mirror body, and while the area is lit one line in the same colour joins it to the body part it moves on the mirror body.
  • A neon flash. An area and the muscles it drives come on at once and flash once, like a neon tube being switched on, the same on the brain area and on both bodies, then glow steadily and fade. There is one flash for each activation and no flicker. While the mouse look keeps your head turned, the neck and trunk areas glow steadily instead of flashing at every turn. When your computer asks for reduced motion, the glow is written to simply rise, with no flash.
  • A comic look, still clinical. The bodies are drawn like a cel-shaded comic in the style of Borderlands: four bands of light from deep shadow to bright highlight, a cool rim, a glossy highlight on muscle and bone, fine ink lines along each muscle and a little ink hatching in the shadows, a few long strokes on tendons and a fine grain on bone. The ink fades on far figures and is off at Low picture quality. The colours are those of an anatomy teaching model, a little stronger: muscles in six tones from rose-salmon to soft red-brown, ivory bones and pearly white where muscles end on bone. Touching muscles get different tones, and nothing looks raw or bloody. The background is space black.
  • Black ink outlines. Smooth black ink lines in the comic style separate your body from the mirror body, and limbs that do not join from each other: a hand over a thigh, a forearm in front of the chest, one leg against the other. They are strongest at silhouettes and overlaps, finer at steps inside a limb and finest where neighbouring muscles meet, and they thin out on small, far figures. They are drawn at double resolution and smoothed, so they do not step or crawl as you look around. At full picture quality, soft shading also darkens the creases between muscles. The outlines stay at every picture quality.
  • Movements that stay. Rest your look on an area for about a third of a second, or click it, and the body part jerks as far as it can go, with a quick spring, and stays there. A quick glance across an area lights it but moves nothing. While a part is held, the opposite area glows faintly, and so do the muscles that pull the other way. Switching that area on brings the part back; Reset body brings everything back.
  • The look reaches an area behind a body. Your look reaches a motor area even when your own body or the mirror body is in front of it at the crosshair. The “Looking at” card names the area and says what is in front of it, and a click switches the area on. To tap the muscle in front instead, hold Shift as you click. Away from the strip, a press acts on what you see at the crosshair. The brain’s glass is clear, so a limb or the mirror body seen through it is what you take, not the glass. Shift with Space, a click or a drag reaches the other thing on the same line of sight.
  • Click any muscle. Clicking a muscle on your own body or on the mirror body switches on the motor area that moves it, and a line names the muscle and the area. Before you click, the “Looking at” card names the muscle, bone, area or arrow under the crosshair.
  • Take hold with Space. Hold Space with the crosshair on the glass brain, the mirror body or one of your limbs. W moves it farther, S closer and A/D sideways, all level with the ground, and your look carries it up and down. The mirror body is carried along the ground only, and W/A/S/D keep it within about 35 degrees of straight ahead. A held hand or foot moves only as far as its joints allow, and the motor areas that would move it light up. Let go of Space to leave it there. A single tap of Space takes hold and keeps holding until the next tap.
  • Press and drag to turn. Drag on the brain or the mirror body to turn it about its own centre by as much as you drag: sideways to turn it round, up or down to tilt it. The mirror body turns through a full circle and tilts as far as the view from straight above or straight below; while it is turned or tilted it stands back along the ground where it needs the room. Drag across one of your limbs to turn it about its length like a dial, inside its range.
  • The limb turn. Tap Space twice and all four limbs turn once: both forearms turn the palms up or down, and both upper arms and both hips turn inward or outward. Each limb carries on in the direction it last turned, so the limbs are not in step. Tap twice and hold and they keep turning, slowly. Let go and every limb stays where it is. Whatever Space held is let go first.
  • Stop at contact. Your body, the glass brain and the mirror body stop a few centimetres short of each other when they are carried, turned, dragged, or moved by a held movement, a trunk bend or the limb turn, and a status line says what stopped. You can still bring them right up to each other and move them around each other. The cases this does not yet cover are listed below.
  • Body Worlds style layers. The outer muscles stand off a little from the deeper ones, outward from the middle of each limb and of the trunk, so the deeper muscles show in the gaps. Their ends stay on the bone, so the parts near the ends are drawn longer than they are. The muscle shapes are closer to the atlas than before, and deeper muscles are drawn a little darker. The layers can be switched off on the Esc page.
  • Solid 3D arrows. Each arrow is a solid V-shaped chevron, tilted with its point away from you. The four grey arrows sit at the middle of each side of a 16:9 frame round the motor strip and turn the brain in place; the bottom one sits a little higher, in the hollow of the strip, to keep clear of the mirror body’s top arrow. The four green arrows sit at the middle of each side of a 9:16 frame round the mirror body: the side ones turn it round, the top and bottom ones tilt it. The frame is the one round the upright mirror body and stays where it is, so a tilted body can reach past it. An arrow is drawn and answers a click only where its target can be seen and your body is not in front of it. The click that captures the mouse does nothing else.
  • A view over your shoulder. E puts the camera above and behind your right shoulder, so the mirror body is in view beside your head. The mouse still turns your head. The brain and your own body keep their size and place; the mirror body slides away by the 1.4 m the camera moved back and returns when you press E again.
  • Forearms without plates. Both forearms twist smoothly along their length when the palm turns, so nothing sticks out at the elbow, palm up or palm down.

The colours and movements are generated by game rules. They are not recordings of a person, electrical stimulation, decoded intent or a validated model of muscle force. A movement staying until its opposite is switched on is a rule of this game, not something measured, and the glow's timing is a display choice, not physiology. The anatomical surfaces come from open atlases; their presence does not establish a precise functional brain map.

Controls

  • Move the mouse or trackpad to look. Your head and eyes turn while your trunk stays put. The game begins when the files load. Click the scene once to capture the pointer.
  • Look at a motor area to see its movements. Click it to do the highlighted movement.
  • Press a number, 1 to 9, to do the movement with that number in the tip beside the crosshair (or on the information card). The list belongs to the area you look at, or the one you looked at last.
  • ← and → put your look on the next motor area toward that side of the screen. Hold a key to keep stepping. The keys wait while Space holds something.
  • Click a muscle, on your body or on the mirror body, to switch on the motor area that moves it. A click on a bone does nothing.
  • W/S move the left hand forward and back; A/D move it left and right. You can combine keys. The strip part and the muscles that make the movement light up.
  • Hold Space on the brain, the mirror body or one of your limbs to take hold. Your left hand closes. Any part of the mirror body takes all of it. Turn your view with the pointer to carry it. W moves it farther, S nearer and A/D sideways. Let go of Space to leave it there. Tap Space to keep holding after the tap, then tap again to let go.
  • Hold Shift as you click, press Space or drag to reach the other thing on the same line of sight.
  • Press and drag on the brain or the mirror body. Drag sideways to turn it or up and down to tilt it. While Space holds it, you can turn it with a drag and carry it with W/A/S/D at the same time. On your own limbs, drag sideways to turn, up to bend and down to straighten. Up and down bring the left hand nearer or farther.
  • Click an arrow for a small turn or step; hold it to keep going. Grey arrows turn the brain in place. Green arrows turn the mirror body round (left and right) or tilt it (top and bottom). Press Esc twice for a free pointer.
  • Tap Space twice and all four limbs turn once at their far ends: the forearms turn the palms up or down and the ankles turn the soles outward or inward; the upper arms and thighs follow by a few degrees. Tap twice and hold and they keep turning, slowly. Let go to leave every limb where it is.
  • E switches between your own eyes and the view over your shoulder. It does nothing while Space holds something; the status card then says to let go first.
  • The game opens with a short reading card and a tip by the crosshair. R opens the full cards and colour key. Press R again to hide the cards, or once more to bring back the short card.
  • Esc pauses and shows the short instructions; “Show the full instructions” on that page has the rest. Reset body, Reset brain view and the settings (look speed, invert Y, picture quality, the lifted outer muscles and the “Try this” guide) are on that page.
  • What is under the crosshair is named in the tip beside it: a motor area with its numbered movements, or a muscle or bone. With the cards on (R), the “Looking at” card at the bottom left names it instead.

Known limitations

  • Asked for on 17 and 18 November and not done yet: the colour code of the strip and the body redone from scratch, with visible links between an area and its part; grouping of every overlay into a few clear chunks; a pass over all earlier notes for small fixes; the rough, pock-marked look of some muscles such as the hip flexors; collision effects and one limb pushing another (your own limbs still pass through each other); a limb hidden behind another limb staying visible; what the measured sweep still shows (see the next entry) and a rule for joint angles in combination; a more detailed, glossy, fleshy graphic-novel look in place of the Borderlands one.
  • Muscles and joints, measured. A script in the repository (node tools/rom-sweep.mjs) poses all 108 movements of the body map and six stacks of movements with the game’s own rig and measures two things against the start posture. Muscle ends: before the muscles were bent with their joints, 1158 muscle ends in 60 of 114 poses stood more than 8 mm off their bone, 153 of them more than 40 mm; now 96 ends in 26 poses, 10 over 20 mm, none over 40 mm. The largest left are two back muscles sliding over the ribs under a raised arm (up to 34 mm) and the lower end of the thigh’s front muscle at a fully bent knee (32 mm from the shin). Joints: no hip, knee, elbow, ankle, wrist or finger joint opens by more than 4 mm; the right shoulder blade lifts off the rib cage by 22 to 37 mm at the far arm positions, and the kneecap ends 21 mm farther from the shin at a fully bent knee. The script measures the game’s own model only: it does not see one part passing through another and does not judge whether joint angles are believable in combination.
  • The slumped posture and the view: the stomach is a narrow band in the start picture and the legs are seen at a flat angle. The view point is 12 cm above the eyes, so the crosshair’s line is parallel to the eyes’ own and not the same line, and a full trunk turn moves the view about 12 cm. The wider field of view stretches the corners. The brain is not tilted to face the eyes; from outside (E) it is a very large shape under and round the body and hides the pelvis and legs from most sides, and whether the legs clear it everywhere was not measured. The left game arm reaches level into the picture. The trunk’s turn and side bend are taken mostly in the low back, more than a real spine gives there. Looking up and far round to the right is closed off: from 155 degrees the look reaches only 30 degrees up. A muscle bent sharply at a joint is drawn thinner there than a real one.
  • The brain is drawn solid since 17 November. Entries below that speak of seeing a body through the brain’s glass were written before that: a limb or the mirror body behind the brain is now hidden by it. Move the brain aside, or hold Shift to reach past it.
  • The changes of 17 November (the loading picture, the quiet screen, holding and moving with the pointer, the mirror body’s limbs, the living brain and its keys, the look all the way round) were checked by their authors with scripted input in one desktop browser only. Nobody else has tried them.
  • The view is 108 degrees from top to bottom. A flat picture that wide stretches what is near its edges: your right arm at the side of a downward look is drawn clearly too large. At far looks to the side the camera turns in the head (the eyes turn), so the drawn head on the mirror body stops turning while your view goes on.
  • The input fixes of 11 November (pointer capture across pause and focus changes, cancelling stale input, a queue for input that arrives between frames) are now part of this version, adapted to the new keys. Scripted tests cover their rules; they have not been tried in several browsers.
  • The developer checks used scripted mouse and key input in desktop Chromium. A separate startup check in Chromium 155 on AMD Vulkan confirmed that Enter on the focused Reload page button reached the first rendered frame after a test anatomy file was restored. This worked with reduced motion off and on. Full gameplay under reduced motion, Firefox, Safari, touch screens, phones and screen readers remain unchecked.
  • The game needs a keyboard and a mouse or trackpad. Window sizes were tried in a desktop browser only. In a window taller than it is wide, the panels cover the lower part of the mirror body; in the view over your shoulder they cover the lower part of both bodies, and the preview badge can sit over the last lines of the panel.
  • Collision checks use spheres that cover the drawn meshes, so items stop a few centimetres apart rather than at their surfaces. Your own body parts are not checked against each other, so one limb can pass through another. A limb's move is checked where it ends in each frame, not along the way. The brain's glass and its inside are kept off the bodies, but the deep cleft between its two halves is not: a brain carried toward you can end with both bodies sitting in that cleft, with glass on both sides. Turning your head is not checked. The checks keep a margin round your left arm's reach, so when the brain or the mirror body rests close to you, a steep look or a trunk bend can stop before anything touches.
  • Moving the crosshair or stepping with ← or → selects a motor area. Click or press one of the numbers to move its body part. The shoulder and hip turns of the limb turn (Space tapped twice) and of a limb dial have no motor area of their own, so only another turn, another dial turn or Reset body brings them back. Two held right-shoulder movements in different planes do not add up: the later one brings the other back to its start. A held head movement moves your view and so carries the crosshair off the area you rested on; the information card keeps that area, and the card at the bottom left says how to bring your head back. After “Tilt head back” and the key that brings the head back, the view ends about 10 degrees above level, below the neck area and not on it.
  • A number key does the whole movement, as a click does. There is no way yet to stop a part half-way or to move it by degrees from the brain. The left shoulder, the tongue and the three in-between areas have no movements, so their cards list none. With ← and →, left and right follow the screen at an ordinary view of the brain; seen exactly from the side the direction is arbitrary. In a small window the short information card leaves out whole parts to fit: at 1280 by 720 most short cards do not name the muscles, and R opens the card that does.
  • Space holds a limb when the crosshair is on one of its muscles or bones, even seen through the brain’s clear glass. Right at the edge of a foot, Space can still take the brain behind it while the view is settling after a quick look. The closed left hand only shows that Space holds something; it does not wrap round what is carried.
  • The toe areas lie on the inner wall between the two halves of the brain and may not face you at the start; turning the brain changes which areas face you, and ← and → pass over areas that are hidden. How many of the 54 areas the crosshair can rest on from the start has not been counted since the brain was moved and made smaller. While you look at the leg end of the strip, the mirror body’s legs are below the picture: look down to watch them move. The labels on the brain hide near the crosshair. The start page is still longer than one screen and scrolls.
  • The lowest face areas, such as the tongue and the throat, lie mostly on the side of the brain. From the start view only their far tips can be aimed at, with the head turned far. A drag or an arrow turns the brain to bring them round, and a line in the status card says so.
  • Some areas and movements have nothing to show. The tongue area has no movement of its own. Your left shoulder already follows your look and the keys; for W, A, S and D the card also names shoulder-blade muscles that this body cannot light. 111 muscles that the earlier anatomy set lacked are now drawn (back, belly, inner thigh, hip, shin, foot and neck), so every movement lights muscles that are there. Over the belly, the sheet of the outer oblique muscle is not drawn where it lies in front of the rectus muscle, as on an atlas plate with the sheath opened; the strip between the two recti is kept. The deep back muscle multifidus is left out because the two atlases do not line up there. Nothing is drawn in place of a missing muscle.
  • The skull is now closed and has eyes, but the top of the head is bare bone: neither atlas has the tendon sheet of the scalp. The muscles at the back of the head are flat sheets in the source, the lips are parted over bone, and there are no muscles at the back of the neck. Where the two atlases' skulls differ, the scalp muscles are lifted onto the bone for display (at most 8.5 mm).
  • The finger and toe bones in the anatomy files end in coarse cones. They are drawn from a smoothed copy with rounded tips (a display change of at most 2.5 mm; the files are unchanged), but at a distance the bare, slightly curled fingers can still read as claws.
  • At strong elbow bends, above about 120 degrees, the inner elbow crease folds in on itself. At looks far to the left and down, your eye is a few centimetres from the left shoulder muscle (deltoid): its front part shows a ragged border, the upper-arm bone shows past it, and during the limb turn a small gap can open between two of its parts.
  • The outlines also outline any faults in the shapes they follow. The gyrus relief of the motor strip is modest at the start view, where the strip is seen mostly from above. Seen through the glass brain, the mirror body looks darker than your own body, and from a distance its belly shows a fine speckle.
  • Only light brighter than white blooms, so the flash on a brain area, which is already lit while you look at it, is fainter than the flash on the muscles. A fast look across the strip lights many areas in a row, each with its own flash. This has not been checked against guidance for people who are sensitive to flashing light. The game is written to drop the flashes when your computer asks for reduced motion. The browser check covered startup and reload. The flashes still need their own check.
  • The arrow tips are placed in pairs at the same height or on the same centre line, but they are fixed points in the scene: looking far to one side tilts a pair by a few pixels, and after a limb turn your own hand or feet can hide one tip of a pair. The mirror body's side tips sit above its centre line, where your left hand does not cover them. Its arrows stay on the frame of the upright body, so a tilted mirror body can reach past them, and at a downward look your own left arm can hide its left arrow. With the left elbow bent, your left hand can cover a brain arrow you aim at with the crosshair, and that arrow then takes no click; use a free pointer (Esc twice) or a drag instead.
  • Muscle and bone names in the status card are the atlas's own names, for example “Distal phalanx of left thumb”.
  • Near the edges of the view things look stretched, as in any wide-angle picture. A mirror body carried close and to one side looks bent.
  • In the author's scripted runs on one desktop computer at 1920 by 1080 pixels and full picture quality, seven runs of the third revision drew 60 frames a second sitting still, 58 to 60 looking around, 55 to 60 during the slow limb turn, 46 to 53 holding the mirror body, 50 to 54 carrying it, and 57 to 58 looking around in the view over your shoulder. Runs differ by several frames a second. Four runs of the second revision made in the same session drew 51 to 53 holding the mirror body and 52 to 54 carrying it; the game’s own work per frame measured the same in both, and no slowdown was found, but a small one is not ruled out. No performance work went into the third revision. Some frames still take longer than a thirtieth of a second, and one took a fifteenth. Before the 12 November changes the same script drew about 34 frames a second while holding the mirror body, about 43 while carrying it with one stall of about a quarter of a second, and had dropped to the roughest picture by its end. With Picture quality: Auto the game switches to a cheaper picture only when that makes the frames faster. Low always draws simplified meshes, which look rougher.
  • Joint ranges are game values within commonly quoted adult ranges, not measured on this body or on anyone. The left game arm stops short of them in places (finger and wrist), its sideways wrist bends only light their area, and its shoulder blade and collarbone slide with the arm rather than rotate. A lifted thigh stops at 110 degrees, where the rigid thigh and belly shapes would start to pass into each other; past 100 degrees the knee is drawn giving way, which is a display rule for the muscles at the back of the thigh and not a measured length. The thigh swings back to 20 degrees behind the line of the trunk. A leg that swings down or back can leave your own view and goes below the level you sit on, where nothing is drawn; watch it on the mirror body. Joint mechanics, muscle deformation, whole-body coupling and the registration of motor areas are simplified and not validated. Some areas stand for overlapping movement controls rather than established stimulation sites. A visible response is illustrative, not proof that a brain location would cause that movement in a person.
