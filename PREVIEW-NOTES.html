Play NeuroSurge · Source code · Desktop launchers

Make the connection visible.

Look at a motor area of the glass brain and the body part it drives moves. Click a muscle and its motor area lights up, in the same colour. NeuroSurge turns that two-way link into a browser game: a skinless body you look out of, a glass brain you can carry and turn, and a mirror body that copies everything you do. Your computer runs the 3D scene and the simulation; the host only supplies the files.

What this preview adds

The Body update, prepared 11 November 2026. It replaces the interaction update of 10 November and has not yet had an independent browser review.

  • Through your own eyes. The picture is taken from between the eyes of the body, and the eyes stay fixed in the head. Moving the mouse turns your head and neck; past their reach your chest turns and your trunk bends, with your legs left on the ground. Your own body is always solid: nothing on it fades or turns see-through when the glass brain is near. Your head and face are behind the picture, so you see them on the mirror body or from behind.
  • A wide view, not an extreme one. The view is 90 degrees from top to bottom, about 121 degrees across a 16:9 window. That is at the top of ordinary game settings: wide enough for the brain and both bodies, without the strong stretching near the edges of a wider view.
  • Sitting with legs and arms forward. You sit upright on an invisible flat ground, with your legs straight out in front, side by side and about a hip width apart, and both arms reaching forward at shoulder height. No chair or floor is drawn. Your legs stay on or above the ground: bending a knee lifts the thigh and slides the heel. The right arm is straight. The left arm is the game arm: its hand keeps a fixed place in your view, so its elbow bends or straightens as you look around.
  • A true mirror body. At the start, and after Reset brain view, the mirror body is the exact reflection of your body in an upright mirror a few centimetres beyond your farthest point: the same size, the same height, on the same ground, never tilted. Your left arm shows on the same side of the screen, as in a real mirror. Like a real reflection seen from your eyes, its head is at eye level, so it does not fill the height of the view.
  • A motor strip that fills the view. At the start the whole brain is brought nearer and scaled so that the motor strip spans the full width of the view. The strip is never moved on the brain: it is the atlas's own precentral gyrus, with the hand areas on the hand knob and the face areas running in order down the side of the brain. Each half now has 27 areas, 54 in all, with an area for each finger, the nose and the throat. Area borders are smooth ink lines, and the labels come in matching pairs on both halves. The strip is drawn in the same comic style as the bodies: light bands across the gyrus, darker banks where it dives into the sulci on either side, an ink line along each sulcus and hatching in the shadows. The fold shading is worked out from the atlas's own brain meshes, a display choice; no folds are invented. The glass brain has inked contours.
  • One colour per area, the same on all three. Every motor area has its own colour, a light or dark shade of its body part's colour family: blues for the left arm, oranges for the right arm, purples for the left leg, yellows for the right leg, greens for belly and back, pinks for face and neck, and grey for the in-between areas, which move nothing. When an area switches on, it and the muscles it drives glow in that colour on your body and on the mirror body, and one short line in the same colour joins the area to its muscles on your own body for about a second.
  • A power-up glow. The glow charges up like a power-up in a game, the same on the brain area and on both bodies, from one shared start: a bright front runs along each muscle from one end to the other, then all three flash at once, hold a steady glow with a slow pulse, and fade over a quarter of a second. A second click on an area that is still glowing charges it again. While the mouse look keeps your head turned, the neck and trunk areas glow steadily instead of flashing at every turn. When your computer asks for reduced motion, the glow is written to simply rise, with no front, flash or pulse.
  • A comic look, still clinical. The bodies are drawn like a cel-shaded comic in the style of Borderlands: four bands of light from deep shadow to bright highlight, a cool rim, fine ink lines along each muscle and ink hatching in the shadows, a few long strokes on tendons and a fine grain on bone. The ink fades on far figures and is off at Low picture quality. The colours are those of an anatomy teaching model, a little stronger: muscles in six tones from rose-salmon to soft red-brown, ivory bones and pearly white where muscles end on bone. Touching muscles get different tones, and nothing looks raw or bloody. The background is space black.
  • Black ink outlines. Smooth black ink lines in the comic style separate your body from the mirror body, and limbs that do not join from each other: a hand over a thigh, a forearm in front of the chest, one leg against the other. They are boldest at silhouettes and overlaps, finer at steps inside a limb and finest where neighbouring muscles meet, and they thin out on small, far figures. They are drawn at double resolution and smoothed, so they do not step or crawl as you look around. At full picture quality, soft shading also darkens the creases between muscles. The outlines stay at every picture quality.
  • Movements that stay. Rest your look on an area for about a third of a second, or click it, and the body part jerks as far as it can go, with a quick spring, and stays there. A quick glance across an area lights it but moves nothing. While a part is held, the opposite area glows faintly, and so do the muscles that pull the other way. Switching that area on brings the part back; Reset body brings everything back.
  • What you see is what you look at. A motor area that your own body or the mirror body covers at the crosshair is not looked at. Looking down, your feet no longer switch on the leg areas behind them, and in the view over your shoulder your back hides the areas behind it. The status card names the muscle or bone in front instead, and a click switches on that muscle's area.
  • Click any muscle. Clicking a muscle on your own body or on the mirror body switches on the motor area that moves it, and a line names the muscle and the area. Before you click, the status card names the muscle, bone, area or arrow under the crosshair.
  • Take hold with F. Hold F with the crosshair on the glass brain, the mirror body or a muscle of one of your limbs. W moves it farther, S closer and A/D sideways, all level with the ground, and your look carries it up and down. The mirror body stays on the ground, and W/A/S/D keep it within about 35 degrees of straight ahead. A held hand or foot moves only as far as its joints allow, and the motor areas that would move it light up. Let go of F to leave it there. Space no longer makes a fist or carries anything.
  • Press and drag to turn. Drag on the brain or the mirror body to turn it about its own centre by as much as you drag. Dragging the mirror body up or down slides it away or back along the ground instead. Drag across one of your limbs to turn it about its length like a dial, inside its range.
  • The Space dance. Hold Space and all four limbs turn slowly in and out: both forearms turn the palms up and down, and both upper arms and both hips turn inward and outward. Each limb has its own tempo and carries on in the direction it last turned, so the limbs are not in step. Tap Space twice and hold for the fast dance. Let go and every limb stays where it is.
  • Stop at contact. Your body, the glass brain and the mirror body stop a few centimetres short of each other when they are carried, turned, dragged, or moved by a held movement, a trunk bend or the dance, and a status line says what stopped. You can still bring them right up to each other and move them around each other. The cases this does not yet cover are listed below.
  • Body Worlds style layers. The outer muscles stand off a little from the deeper ones, outward from the middle of each limb and of the trunk, so the deeper muscles show in the gaps. Their ends stay on the bone, so the parts near the ends are drawn longer than they are. The muscle shapes are closer to the atlas than before, and deeper muscles are drawn a little darker. The layers can be switched off on the Esc page.
  • Solid 3D arrows. Each arrow is a solid V-shaped chevron, tilted with its point away from you. The four grey arrows sit at the middle of each side of a 16:9 frame round the motor strip and turn the brain in place. The four green arrows sit at the middle of each side of a 9:16 frame round the mirror body: the side ones turn it, the top and bottom ones slide it away and back along the ground. An arrow is drawn and answers a click only where its target can be seen and your body is not in front of it. The click that captures the mouse does nothing else.
  • A view over your shoulder. E puts the camera above and behind your right shoulder, so the mirror body is in view beside your head. The mouse still turns your head, and the brain and both bodies keep the same size and place as in your own view.
  • Forearms without plates. Both forearms twist smoothly along their length when the palm turns, so nothing sticks out at the elbow, palm up or palm down.

The colours and movements are generated by game rules. They are not recordings of a person, electrical stimulation, decoded intent or a validated model of muscle force. A movement staying until its opposite is switched on is a rule of this game, not something measured, and the glow's timing is a display choice, not physiology. The anatomical surfaces come from open atlases; their presence does not establish a precise functional brain map.

Controls

  • Move the mouse or trackpad to look. It turns your head and neck, then your chest and trunk. Click the game once to capture the pointer; that click does nothing else.
  • Rest your look on a motor area for about a third of a second, or click it: that body part moves as far as it can go and stays there.
  • Click a muscle, on your body or on the mirror body, to switch on the motor area that moves it. A click on a bone does nothing.
  • W/S move the left hand forward and back; A/D move it left and right. You can combine keys.
  • Hold F on the brain, the mirror body or a muscle of one of your limbs to take hold of it. While F is held, W moves it farther, S closer, A/D sideways, and your look carries it up and down; the mirror body stays on the ground. Let go of F to leave it there.
  • Press on the brain or the mirror body and drag to turn it; drag the mirror body up or down to slide it away or back. Drag across one of your limbs to turn it like a dial.
  • Click an arrow for a small turn or step; hold it to keep going. Grey arrows turn the brain in place. Green arrows turn the mirror body (left and right) or slide it along the ground (top and bottom). Press Esc twice for a free pointer.
  • Hold Space for the slow dance of all four limbs. Tap Space twice and hold for the fast dance. Let go to leave every limb where it is.
  • E switches between your own eyes and the view over your shoulder. It does nothing while F holds something; the status card then says to let go of F first.
  • I shows or hides More detail in the panel and the colour key.
  • Esc pauses and shows the instructions. Reset body, Reset brain view and the settings (look speed, invert Y, picture quality, the lifted outer muscles and the “Try this” guide) are on that page.

Known limitations

  • The input fixes published on 11 November (pointer capture across pause and focus changes, cancelling stale input, a queue for input that arrives between frames) are not in this version yet; they are being brought across. Until then, rare orders of pause, focus and key events may leave a key or a drag held.
  • This build has only been run in one desktop browser (Chromium, with scripted mouse and key input and screenshots), by its author and by other instances of the same model that wrote it, which also read the code; what they found was fixed or is listed here. That is not an independent review, which is still to come. Firefox, Safari, touch screens, phones, screen readers and reduced-motion settings are unchecked.
  • The game needs a keyboard and a mouse or trackpad. Window sizes were tried in a desktop browser only. In a window taller than it is wide, the panels cover the lower part of the mirror body; in the view over your shoulder they cover the lower part of both bodies, and the preview badge can sit over the last lines of the panel.
  • Collision checks use spheres that cover the drawn meshes, so items stop a few centimetres apart rather than at their surfaces. Your own body parts are not checked against each other, so one limb can pass through another. A limb's move is checked where it ends in each frame, not along the way. The brain's glass and its inside are kept off the bodies, but the deep cleft between its two halves is not: a brain carried toward you can end with both bodies sitting in that cleft, with glass on both sides. Turning your head is not checked. The checks keep a margin round your left arm's reach, so when the brain or the mirror body rests close to you, a steep look or a trunk bend can stop before anything touches.
  • Because the motor strip fills the view, the crosshair crosses many areas while you look around. Resting on one for about a third of a second moves that body part, and it stays moved. The shoulder and hip turns of the Space dance and of a limb dial have no motor area of their own, so only another dance, another dial turn or Reset body brings them back. Two held right-shoulder movements in different planes do not add up: the later one brings the other back to its start. A held neck movement adds to your look.
  • F holds a limb when the crosshair is on one of its muscles or bones, even seen through the brain's clear glass. Right at the edge of a foot, F can still take the brain behind it while the view is settling after a quick look.
  • At the start the mirror body and your own feet sit in front of the leg end of the motor strip. In a scripted sweep of looks from the start, at 2-degree steps, the crosshair came to rest on 50 of the 54 areas. The two toe areas, which lie on the inner wall between the two halves, and the lowest in-between area on each side were never reached. Turning the brain changes which areas face you.
  • The lowest face areas, such as the tongue and the throat, lie mostly on the side of the brain. From the start view only their far tips can be aimed at, with the head turned far. A drag or an arrow turns the brain to bring them round, and a line in the status card says so.
  • Some areas and movements have nothing to show. The tongue area and the left shoulder area have no movement of their own; your left shoulder already follows your look and the keys. 111 muscles that the earlier anatomy set lacked are now drawn (back, belly, inner thigh, hip, shin, foot and neck), so every movement lights muscles that are there. Over the belly, the sheet of the outer oblique muscle is not drawn where it lies in front of the rectus muscle, as on an atlas plate with the sheath opened; the strip between the two recti is kept. The deep back muscle multifidus is left out because the two atlases do not line up there. Nothing is drawn in place of a missing muscle.
  • The skull is now closed and has eyes, but the top of the head is bare bone: neither atlas has the tendon sheet of the scalp. The muscles at the back of the head are flat sheets in the source, the lips are parted over bone, and there are no muscles at the back of the neck. Where the two atlases' skulls differ, the scalp muscles are lifted onto the bone for display (at most 8.5 mm).
  • The finger and toe bones in the anatomy files end in coarse cones. They are drawn from a smoothed copy with rounded tips (a display change of at most 2.5 mm; the files are unchanged), but at a distance the bare, slightly curled fingers can still read as claws.
  • At strong elbow bends, above about 120 degrees, the inner elbow crease folds in on itself. During the dance the left shoulder muscle (deltoid) can show a pointed edge near the camera.
  • The outlines also outline any faults in the shapes they follow. The gyrus relief of the motor strip is modest at the start view, where the strip is seen mostly from above. Seen through the glass brain, the mirror body looks darker than your own body, and from a distance its belly shows a fine speckle.
  • Only light brighter than white blooms, so the flash on a brain area, which is already lit while you look at it, is fainter than the flash on the muscles. A fast look across the strip lights many areas in a row, each with its own flash. This has not been checked against guidance for people who are sensitive to flashing light. The game is written to drop the flashes when your computer asks for reduced motion, but that has not been tried in a browser.
  • The arrow tips are placed in pairs at the same height or on the same centre line, but they are fixed points in the scene: looking far to one side tilts a pair by a few pixels, and after the Space dance your own hand or feet can hide one tip of a pair. The mirror body's side tips sit above its centre line, where your left hand does not cover them. With the left elbow bent, your left hand can cover a brain arrow you aim at with the crosshair, and that arrow then takes no click; use a free pointer (Esc twice) or a drag instead.
  • Muscle and bone names in the status card are the atlas's own names, for example “Distal phalanx of left thumb”.
  • Near the edges of the view things look stretched, as in any wide-angle picture. A mirror body carried close and to one side looks bent.
  • In the author's single-core test runs at 1600 by 900 pixels, standing still drew 60 frames a second, but the Space dance drew about 37, looking around about 39 and carrying the mirror body about 39, with some frames taking longer than a thirtieth of a second. On a slow computer, Picture quality: Auto or Low draws simplified meshes, which look rougher.
  • Joint ranges are game values within commonly quoted adult ranges, not measured on this body or on anyone. The left game arm stops short of them in places (finger and wrist), its sideways wrist bends only light their area, and its shoulder blade and collarbone slide with the arm rather than rotate. A lifted thigh stops at 110 degrees, where the rigid thigh and belly shapes would start to pass into each other. Because your heel also stays on the ground, a knee bend from the start goes only part of the way: the knee rises a little instead of folding fully. Joint mechanics, muscle deformation, whole-body coupling and the registration of motor areas are simplified and not validated. Some areas stand for overlapping movement controls rather than established stimulation sites. A visible response is illustrative, not proof that a brain location would cause that movement in a person.
