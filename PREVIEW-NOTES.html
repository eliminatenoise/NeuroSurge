Make the connection visible.

Look at a motor area of the glass brain and the body part it drives moves, in the same colours. Click a muscle and its motor area lights up. NeuroSurge turns that two-way link into a browser game: a skinless body you look out of, a brain you can turn in your hands, and a mirror body that copies everything you do. Your computer runs the 3D scene and the simulation; the host only supplies the files.

What this preview adds

The interaction update, prepared 10 November 2026. It has not yet had an independent browser review.

  • It no longer stops. In the camera preview, looking far up, far down or far sideways, or letting go of a grasp near the arm's limit, could stop the game. The view and the arm now simply hold at the last position that can be shown. If the arm cannot follow your look any further, a short line says so.
  • Smoother. Standing still at the start view went from about 23 to about 60 frames a second in our test; looking around gently, from about 12 to about 35; reaching, from about 24 to about 56. These were measured in one browser on one processor core, with the same scripted mouse and key input for both versions. Your computer will differ.
  • Turn things by dragging. Press on the brain and drag: it turns about its centre by as much as you drag. The mirror body turns the same way once the brain is out of its way.
  • Click a muscle. Click a muscle on your own body or on the mirror body. The motor area that moves it switches on for one second, the muscles of that movement light up on both bodies, and a line names the muscle and the area. You can click the mirror body's muscles through the clear glass of the brain.
  • New arrows. The four arrows around the brain, and the four around the mirror body, are now wide see-through paper ribbons with a broad tail, a narrow neck and a pointed head that curls away from you. They stay where they are when you click them, turn the brain or look around. A motor area is always drawn over an arrow, never under it.
  • The brain sits higher. The whole brain moved up as one piece, so the leg areas at the bottom of the U are near the middle of the view.
  • Motor areas you can read. The areas are more solid and shaded like soft tissue. Left-arm areas are light blue; every other body part is now pink instead of grey. The rest of the brain stays glass.
  • Smoother muscles and bones. The face, neck and torso muscles and the skull were lit as a patchwork because the anatomy files repeat points along their seams. Those repeated points now share their shading. No anatomy was added, moved or filled in.
  • Palms, forearms and legs turn together. Tap Space twice: both forearms turn palm up or palm down, and both hips turn the legs and feet a little outward with them.
  • A picture of the keys. The start page and the Esc page show where W, A, S, D, E, I, Space and Esc sit on a keyboard, with what the mouse or trackpad does.
  • No floating face. The see-through copy of your own face that appeared when you looked up is gone. The mirror body shows the face.

The colours and movements are generated by game rules. They are not recordings of a person, electrical stimulation, decoded intent or a validated model of muscle force. The anatomical surfaces come from open atlases; their presence does not establish a precise functional brain map.

Controls

  • Move the mouse or trackpad to look. Click the game once to capture the pointer.
  • W/S move the left hand forward and back; A/D move it left and right. You can combine keys.
  • Hold Space to close all five digits; release to open. While looking at the brain, or at the mirror body when the brain is not in front of it, hold Space to carry that item: move the mouse to reposition it, W moves it farther away, S brings it closer, A/D move it sideways. Release Space to leave it there.
  • Tap Space twice to turn your palms, forearms and legs.
  • Look at a motor area to switch it on for one second. Click to switch it on again.
  • Click a muscle, on your body or on the mirror body, to switch on the motor area that moves it.
  • Press on the brain and drag to turn it. Press on the mirror body and drag to turn it, once the brain is aside.
  • Click or hold a ribbon arrow for the same turns in small steps.
  • E switches between your own eyes and a view from behind. I opens More detail in the panel.
  • Esc opens the instructions and pauses. Reset body and Reset brain view are on that page.

Known limitations

  • This build has only been run by its author in one desktop browser (Chromium, with scripted mouse and key input and screenshots). Firefox, Safari, touch screens, phones and screen readers are unchecked. Independent review is still to come.
  • Your look stops where the whole left arm can no longer be shown: about 40 degrees to either side at the start pose (more or less in other poses), and nearly straight up or down. In one rare case in our tests, pushing the arm forward while looking far to the side paused the picture for about a third of a second and then turned the view part of the way back.
  • Looking around is the slowest thing the game does: about 35 frames a second in our single-core test, with single frames as long as a tenth of a second.
  • The keys do not move the arm farther than before. W already ends at the arm's full length, S where the whole hand still fits in view, and A/D at the edge of the view and at the information panel. A wider sweep would need the hand to leave its fixed height on screen.
  • A clicked muscle switches on a whole motor area and all the muscles of that area's movement, not that one muscle alone. A muscle the game has no movement for only lights its body part's area. The left shoulder area has no movement of its own, because your left shoulder already follows your look and the keys. Bones cannot be switched on.
  • In the leg turn only the hips rotate, by 10 degrees, and the muscles that would do it are not coloured. The right upper arm does not turn. The left shoulder blade and collarbone now slide with the arm on both bodies when you look up or reach across; it is a slide, not a worked-out shoulder-blade rotation.
  • The top arrow of the brain is partly above the screen at the start view on a wide screen, and the left arrow can sit behind your own arm (a faint outline shows through). Dragging does the same job.
  • Outer muscles are still lifted off the deeper ones on purpose, so real gaps between layers remain. On a slow computer, Picture quality: Auto or Low draws simplified meshes, which can look rough again.
  • Joint mechanics, muscle deformation, whole-body coupling and the registration of motor areas remain simplified and are not validated. Some areas stand for overlapping movement controls rather than established stimulation sites. A visible response is illustrative, not proof that a brain location would cause that movement in a person.
