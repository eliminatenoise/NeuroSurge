Follow a movement through the map.

A coloured brain area starts the game's linked movement. A muscle click takes the link the other way. The body surfaces come from human anatomy atlases; the activity values are simulated and unitless. No neural recording or trained decoder drives this preview. A selected movement group is not a precise brain site for an individual muscle.

Package edition: experimental-2026-11-11-pointer-release. Your computer runs the rendering, controls and simulation after the browser downloads the files. JavaScript, WebGL, a keyboard and a mouse or trackpad are required.

When a release goes missing

A missing mouse-up event used to leave a drag or arrow hold armed, so the next press could be lost. The game now cancels that old gesture as soon as the browser reports no buttons held. A new press starts a new gesture. Cancellation adds no click and leaves held Space/WASD keys alone; Esc and focus loss still release them.

Independent software tests passed all 40 named cases with the repair; 20 failed against the previous controller. They use in-memory event records, not a physical mouse or a rendered scene. They cannot recover the missing release time or establish display latency. A naturally missed mouse-up event still needs an ordinary browser capture. The self-contained test suite is included with the source.

A desktop Chromium check of this package observed two right-arrow presses and releases, Esc pause/resume, Space held through a buttonless mouse movement and then released, and E in both directions. All ten sampled states stayed running without a reported error. No naturally missed mouse-up or input-to-display latency was established.

Earlier control-build observations

One desktop Chromium check recorded seven ordinary states: initial play, Esc pause/resume, Space held/released and first-to-third-to-first view changes. They stayed running without a reported error, with current calculated-state readbacks. Rapid Esc resume was observed within about 212.9 ms of pointer unlock, using a commanded 50 ms gap between presses. That does not pass the 100 ms input-to-displayed-frame requirement or establish every keyboard event order.

Two ordinary right-arrow clicks rotated the brain. Its recorded centre and all four nominal CPU arrow positions stayed exactly unchanged in that case. This is a narrow orbit observation, not proof of stable pixels, every compass control or every view. The current pictures still show patchy, jagged muscles, crowded body/brain placement and a slow-render warning. Independent hardware frame rates remain unmeasured.

Gaze / click a brain areaMoving onto a new area starts a one-second game pulse; click to repeat. Exact timing remains under review. A held fist can take precedence over hand-area responses.

The startup instructions are set to close two seconds after the body loads. Esc gives you time to read them. Reset body requests the starting pose and clears activation marks while keeping the brain's placement. Reset brain view returns the brain and turns the mirror to face you. A persistent activation mark is separate from the brief pulse.

The ordinary view shows your whole left arm, lap and legs while hiding the back of your trunk. Use the mirror for its front and E for its back and head. The separate floating face stays disabled. Turn the brain to expose areas around its sides.

Limits that affect play

  • The game can hold or retreat a look/reach when the arm cannot fit. It can still fail. If E cannot complete from a pose, try looking toward the middle or moving the hand forward a little.
  • Waiting input keeps only its first 64 events, so a later release can be lost. Release recovery can substitute a safety release without its original time. A pause between updates can replay deferred input. Other missing-input cases remain under review. Reload if the hand stays closed or the scene stops responding.
  • A late failure can leave the simulated state, calculated output and displayed scene out of step. The current check did not exercise natural recovery, overflow, missing-release or late-failure cases. Camera rollback covers the whole-arm route only.
  • Near/far endpoints, combined-key carrying, whole-arm borders and the hand's upper-quarter screen position need broader checks. The mirror's larger apparent size has not established that it starts physically closer.
  • Arrows may be hidden by the player's arm, close to an edge or overlap one another. Dragging provides another way to turn the object. Full motor-area clearance and pixel stability remain unverified.
  • Portrait layout is unfinished. A prior composition failed at 375 × 667 and was crowded at 390 × 844; this package has no new portrait acceptance. Use a wider desktop window. Touch, phones and tablets remain unverified.
  • The projection can stretch the brain at steep angles. The first-person clipping is a display compromise rather than a literal eye-level camera. Surface shading changes have not removed all splotches or jagged edges.
  • The right upper-arm turn and matching hip-muscle colours are incomplete. The shoulder blade and collarbone use a simplified slide. Joint mechanics, muscle deformation, own/mirror coupling and motor-area registration remain unvalidated against a complete real-reference series.
  • Try Picture quality: Low if rendering is slow; it can make surfaces rougher. No independently established hardware frame rate or speed gain is claimed.
  • Other browsers, screen readers, reduced-motion behavior and native Windows/macOS opening remain unverified. Earlier Linux launch evidence does not verify the updated online target.

The original exact timing, full-control, anatomical, held-out functional and output/export checks remain incomplete. A visible response illustrates the game's rules; it does not establish decoded intent, neural stimulation, muscle force or clinical function. Design decisions explain the mechanisms and the remaining gaps.
