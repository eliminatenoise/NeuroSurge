Follow a movement through the map.

A coloured brain area starts the game's linked movement. A muscle click takes the link the other way. The body surfaces come from human anatomy atlases; the activity values are simulated and unitless. No neural recording or trained decoder drives this preview. A selected movement group is not a precise brain site for an individual muscle.

Package edition: experimental-2026-11-11-input-queue. Your computer runs the rendering, controls and simulation after the browser downloads the files. JavaScript, WebGL, a keyboard and a mouse or trackpad are required.

Keep the end of the queue

A refused movement frame can leave input waiting for another attempt. Within a combined bound of 64 pending game-event records, the game now retains the complete list with its original times and order. Over the bound, it cancels that waiting input, marks one safety release at the observed cancellation time and asks you to release and press again. This deliberately drops old commands rather than silently truncating the queue.

The check happens before input copying, simulation-time mapping, camera changes and view planning. Long-skip rescue also uses explicit cancellation. Its previous reset and stop thresholds remain. The earlier click-recording limit is separate; these changes do not prove capture or delivery of every physical action.

Independent software testing passed 11 new named cases, plus all 13 cancellation and 40 pointer regression cases. The supplied tests use event records and plain in-memory listener containers, without loading the application, renderer or model. The new suite includes boundaries, release-then-new-press ordering, repeated retention and guarded declared-count checks. It does not process a billion actual inputs or establish a native timing result.

A combined-key problem remains

In one ordinary browser sequence soon after capture, Space unexpectedly stopped registering when W and D were pressed. This happened in both the candidate runtime and the preceding live build, without the new overflow handler firing. Later staggered or unlocked sequences worked. The cause is unknown. Full simultaneous Space/WASD behavior remains unverified; release all keys and try again if the hand opens unexpectedly.

Space alone, arrow hold/release, both E directions and a modifier safety release were also observed. Fourteen sampled candidate states kept running without a reported game error, but the combined-key observation above was a failure. A running scene is not evidence that every required input worked. Native overflow, long-skip rescue and full event-capture coverage remain open.

Earlier pause-update observations

Pause without replay

Pausing clears queued clicks, object turns and camera-switch requests immediately. Resuming clears them again, so a gesture made behind the instructions cannot leak into play. Focus changes, resets and observed graphics-context changes also cancel pending input. The game records the cancellation time and queues one marked safety release for the normal simulation step; it does not invent the time of a missing physical release.

Old carry anchors are cleared while saved placement stays intact. In one desktop Chromium check of this repaired runtime, a carried brain kept exactly the same saved position across pause and resume. An interrupted arrow hold stopped; the next fresh click turned the brain once. Camera changes worked both ways; the visible Reset body command reached the cancellation boundary and play resumed. All 15 sampled states stayed running without a reported game error.

Independent pure software testing passed 13 named cases, including 128 keyboard-state combinations. It checks the public input reducer and cancellation helpers, without loading the full application, browser or model. The test source is included. Rare pause/focus/lifecycle orders, a pause without an intervening animation callback, and graphics loss/restoration remain without complete native coverage. No physical display-timing result follows.

When a release goes missing

A missing mouse-up event used to leave a drag or arrow hold armed, so the next press could be lost. The game now cancels that old gesture as soon as the browser reports no buttons held. A new press starts a new gesture. Cancellation adds no click and leaves held Space/WASD keys alone; Esc and focus loss still release them.

For the earlier pointer-release controller, independent software tests passed all 40 named cases; 20 failed against the previous controller. They use in-memory event records, not a physical mouse or a rendered scene. They cannot recover the missing release time or establish display latency. A naturally missed mouse-up event still needs an ordinary browser capture. The self-contained test suite is included with the source. All 40 pointer cases were rerun with the current controller and passed; this remains software-state evidence.

A desktop Chromium check of the prior pointer package observed two right-arrow presses and releases, Esc pause/resume, Space held through a buttonless mouse movement and then released, and E in both directions. All ten sampled states stayed running without a reported error. No naturally missed mouse-up or input-to-display latency was established.

Earlier control-build observations

One desktop Chromium check recorded seven ordinary states: initial play, Esc pause/resume, Space held/released and first-to-third-to-first view changes. They stayed running without a reported error, with current calculated-state readbacks. Rapid Esc resume was observed within about 212.9 ms of pointer unlock, using a commanded 50 ms gap between presses. That does not pass the 100 ms input-to-displayed-frame requirement or establish every keyboard event order.

Two ordinary right-arrow clicks rotated the brain. Its recorded centre and all four nominal CPU arrow positions stayed exactly unchanged in that case. This is a narrow orbit observation, not proof of stable pixels, every compass control or every view. The current pictures still show patchy, jagged muscles, crowded body/brain placement and a slow-render warning. Independent hardware frame rates remain unmeasured.

The startup instructions are set to close two seconds after the body loads. Esc gives you time to read them. Reset body requests the starting pose and clears activation marks while keeping the brain's placement. Reset brain view returns the brain and turns the mirror to face you. A persistent activation mark is separate from the brief pulse.

The ordinary view shows your whole left arm, lap and legs while hiding the back of your trunk. Use the mirror for its front and E for its back and head. The separate floating face stays disabled. Turn the brain to expose areas around its sides.

Limits that affect play

  • The game can hold or retreat a look/reach when the arm cannot fit. It can still fail. If E cannot complete from a pose, try looking toward the middle or moving the hand forward a little.
  • The combined pending limit now cancels an overfull queue explicitly. Long-skip rescue uses a separately marked, observed-time safety release; it does not preserve a canceled original event as a newly timed real release. The new cancellation boundary is source-reviewed; rare pause/focus/lifecycle orders and other missing-input cases still need broader browser checks. Reload if the hand stays closed or the scene stops responding.
  • A late failure can leave the simulated state, calculated output and displayed scene out of step. The current check did not exercise natural recovery, overflow, missing-release or late-failure cases. The fast combined-key failure remains unresolved. Camera rollback covers the whole-arm route only.
  • Near/far endpoints, combined-key carrying, whole-arm borders and the hand's upper-quarter screen position need broader checks. The mirror's larger apparent size has not established that it starts physically closer.
  • Arrows may be hidden by the player's arm, close to an edge or overlap one another. Dragging provides another way to turn the object. Full motor-area clearance and pixel stability remain unverified.
  • Portrait layout is unfinished. A prior composition failed at 375 × 667 and was crowded at 390 × 844; this package has no new portrait acceptance. Use a wider desktop window. Touch, phones and tablets remain unverified.
  • The projection can stretch the brain at steep angles. The first-person clipping is a display compromise rather than a literal eye-level camera. Surface shading changes have not removed all splotches or jagged edges.
  • The right upper-arm turn and matching hip-muscle colours are incomplete. The shoulder blade and collarbone use a simplified slide. Joint mechanics, muscle deformation, own/mirror coupling and motor-area registration remain unvalidated against a complete real-reference series.
  • Try Picture quality: Low if rendering is slow; it can make surfaces rougher. No independently established hardware frame rate or speed gain is claimed.
  • Other browsers, screen readers, reduced-motion behavior and native Windows/macOS opening remain unverified. Earlier Linux launch evidence does not verify the updated online target.

The original exact timing, full-control, anatomical, held-out functional and output/export checks remain incomplete. A visible response illustrates the game's rules; it does not establish decoded intent, neural stimulation, muscle force or clinical function. Design decisions explain the mechanisms and the remaining gaps.
