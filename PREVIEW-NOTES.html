Play NeuroSurge · Source code · Desktop launchers

Make the connection visible.

Look at a motor area of the glass brain and the body part it drives moves. Click a muscle and its motor area lights up, in the same colour. NeuroSurge turns that two-way link into a browser game: a skinless body you look out of, a glass brain you can carry and turn, and a mirror body that copies everything you do. Your computer runs the 3D scene and the simulation; the host only supplies the files.

What this preview adds

The Body update, prepared 11 November 2026 and revised on 12 November. It replaces the interaction update of 10 November and has not yet had an independent browser review.

Changed on 12 November

  • Space is the one hand key. Hold Space to take hold of what you look at. Tap it to take hold and keep holding, and tap again to let go. Tap twice and all four limbs turn once; tap twice and hold and they keep turning, slowly. The F key is no longer used. While a hold lasts, your left hand closes, the hand part of the left-arm strip lights and the crosshair shows a ring. The closed hand is a sign of the hold only: its fingers do not touch what is carried.
  • Each activation shows what belongs together. W, A, S and D light only the shoulder part of the left-arm strip, with the elbow part for W and S, and the muscles that make that movement, instead of the whole arm region. While an area is lit, one line in its colour joins it to the body part it moves on the mirror body. The information card names the key, the strip part, the movement and the muscles. The muscles listed for each direction are the usual textbook movers, lit at a made-up level.
  • R reads the information. R replaces I. The side cards are on at the start. R adds More detail and the colour key, R again hides all of it, and R again brings the cards back. The status card is shorter than before.
  • The brain takes about half the width. The motor strip spans about half the width of the view instead of all of it, which leaves room for both bodies beside it.
  • A neon flash instead of a power-up. An area and its muscles come on at once and flash once, like a neon tube being switched on, then glow steadily. The glow answers within a twentieth of a second.
  • Gloss, and lighter ink. Muscles, tendons, bones and motor areas were matte and now carry a glossy highlight. The comic ink is toned down: thinner, lighter outlines and much less hatching and stipple.
  • Slightly bent limbs. A stretched right elbow keeps about 12 degrees of bend and each knee about 10. Only the drawing changes: the joint angles and limits still count from straight, and the heels stay on the ground. The left arm already kept a small bend at full reach.
  • The mirror body keeps its distance in the view over your shoulder. E moves the camera 1.4 m behind you. The mirror body now slides away by the same distance and comes back when you press E again.
  • Faster, with the same picture. Holding and carrying the brain or the mirror body, the pose of the body, the skinning of the left arm and the drawing of the brain’s glass take less work in each frame and give the same picture. With Picture quality: Auto, the game now switches to a cheaper picture only when that makes the frames faster. Frame rates are under Known limitations.

From 11 November

  • Through your own eyes. The picture is taken from between the eyes of the body, and the eyes stay fixed in the head. Moving the mouse turns your head and neck; past their reach your chest turns and your trunk bends, with your legs left on the ground. Your own body is always solid: nothing on it fades or turns see-through when the glass brain is near. Your head and face are behind the picture, so you see them on the mirror body or from behind.
  • A wide view, not an extreme one. The view is 90 degrees from top to bottom, about 121 degrees across a 16:9 window. That is at the top of ordinary game settings: wide enough for the brain and both bodies, without the strong stretching near the edges of a wider view.
  • Sitting with legs and arms forward. You sit upright on an invisible flat ground, with your legs out in front, side by side and about a hip width apart, and both arms reaching forward at shoulder height. No chair or floor is drawn. Your legs stay on or above the ground: bending a knee lifts the thigh and slides the heel. A stretched elbow or knee keeps a slight bend. The left arm is the game arm: its hand keeps a fixed place in your view, so its elbow bends or straightens as you look around.
  • A true mirror body. At the start, and after Reset brain view, the mirror body is the exact reflection of your body in an upright mirror a few centimetres beyond your farthest point: the same size, the same height, on the same ground, never tilted. Your left arm shows on the same side of the screen, as in a real mirror. Like a real reflection seen from your eyes, its head is at eye level, so it does not fill the height of the view.
  • A motor strip across half the view. At the start the whole brain is brought nearer and scaled so that the motor strip spans about half the width of the view. The strip is never moved on the brain: it is the atlas's own precentral gyrus, with the hand areas on the hand knob and the face areas running in order down the side of the brain. Each half now has 27 areas, 54 in all, with an area for each finger, the nose and the throat. Area borders are smooth ink lines, and the labels come in matching pairs on both halves. The strip is drawn in the same comic style as the bodies: light bands across the gyrus, darker banks where it dives into the sulci on either side, an ink line along each sulcus and hatching in the shadows. The fold shading is worked out from the atlas's own brain meshes, a display choice; no folds are invented. The glass brain has inked contours.
  • One colour per area, the same on all three. Every motor area has its own colour, a light or dark shade of its body part's colour family: blues for the left arm, oranges for the right arm, purples for the left leg, yellows for the right leg, greens for belly and back, pinks for face and neck, and grey for the in-between areas, which move nothing. When an area switches on, it and the muscles it drives glow in that colour on your body and on the mirror body, and while the area is lit one line in the same colour joins it to the body part it moves on the mirror body.
  • A neon flash. An area and the muscles it drives come on at once and flash once, like a neon tube being switched on, the same on the brain area and on both bodies, then glow steadily and fade. There is one flash for each activation and no flicker. While the mouse look keeps your head turned, the neck and trunk areas glow steadily instead of flashing at every turn. When your computer asks for reduced motion, the glow is written to simply rise, with no flash.
  • A comic look, still clinical. The bodies are drawn like a cel-shaded comic in the style of Borderlands: four bands of light from deep shadow to bright highlight, a cool rim, a glossy highlight on muscle and bone, fine ink lines along each muscle and a little ink hatching in the shadows, a few long strokes on tendons and a fine grain on bone. The ink fades on far figures and is off at Low picture quality. The colours are those of an anatomy teaching model, a little stronger: muscles in six tones from rose-salmon to soft red-brown, ivory bones and pearly white where muscles end on bone. Touching muscles get different tones, and nothing looks raw or bloody. The background is space black.
  • Black ink outlines. Smooth black ink lines in the comic style separate your body from the mirror body, and limbs that do not join from each other: a hand over a thigh, a forearm in front of the chest, one leg against the other. They are strongest at silhouettes and overlaps, finer at steps inside a limb and finest where neighbouring muscles meet, and they thin out on small, far figures. They are drawn at double resolution and smoothed, so they do not step or crawl as you look around. At full picture quality, soft shading also darkens the creases between muscles. The outlines stay at every picture quality.
  • Movements that stay. Rest your look on an area for about a third of a second, or click it, and the body part jerks as far as it can go, with a quick spring, and stays there. A quick glance across an area lights it but moves nothing. While a part is held, the opposite area glows faintly, and so do the muscles that pull the other way. Switching that area on brings the part back; Reset body brings everything back.
  • The look reaches an area behind a body. Your look reaches a motor area even when your own body or the mirror body is in front of it at the crosshair. The “Looking at” card names the area and says what is in front of it, and a click switches the area on. To tap the muscle in front instead, hold Shift as you click. Away from the strip, a press acts on what you see at the crosshair. The brain’s glass is clear, so a limb or the mirror body seen through it is what you take, not the glass. Shift with Space, a click or a drag reaches the other thing on the same line of sight.
  • Click any muscle. Clicking a muscle on your own body or on the mirror body switches on the motor area that moves it, and a line names the muscle and the area. Before you click, the “Looking at” card names the muscle, bone, area or arrow under the crosshair.
  • Take hold with Space. Hold Space with the crosshair on the glass brain, the mirror body or one of your limbs. W moves it farther, S closer and A/D sideways, all level with the ground, and your look carries it up and down. The mirror body stays on the ground, and W/A/S/D keep it within about 35 degrees of straight ahead. A held hand or foot moves only as far as its joints allow, and the motor areas that would move it light up. Let go of Space to leave it there. A single tap of Space takes hold and keeps holding until the next tap.
  • Press and drag to turn. Drag on the brain or the mirror body to turn it about its own centre by as much as you drag. Dragging the mirror body up or down slides it away or back along the ground instead. Drag across one of your limbs to turn it about its length like a dial, inside its range.
  • The limb turn. Tap Space twice and all four limbs turn once: both forearms turn the palms up or down, and both upper arms and both hips turn inward or outward. Each limb carries on in the direction it last turned, so the limbs are not in step. Tap twice and hold and they keep turning, slowly. Let go and every limb stays where it is. Whatever Space held is let go first.
  • Stop at contact. Your body, the glass brain and the mirror body stop a few centimetres short of each other when they are carried, turned, dragged, or moved by a held movement, a trunk bend or the limb turn, and a status line says what stopped. You can still bring them right up to each other and move them around each other. The cases this does not yet cover are listed below.
  • Body Worlds style layers. The outer muscles stand off a little from the deeper ones, outward from the middle of each limb and of the trunk, so the deeper muscles show in the gaps. Their ends stay on the bone, so the parts near the ends are drawn longer than they are. The muscle shapes are closer to the atlas than before, and deeper muscles are drawn a little darker. The layers can be switched off on the Esc page.
  • Solid 3D arrows. Each arrow is a solid V-shaped chevron, tilted with its point away from you. The four grey arrows sit at the middle of each side of a 16:9 frame round the motor strip and turn the brain in place. The four green arrows sit at the middle of each side of a 9:16 frame round the mirror body: the side ones turn it, the top and bottom ones slide it away and back along the ground. An arrow is drawn and answers a click only where its target can be seen and your body is not in front of it. The click that captures the mouse does nothing else.
  • A view over your shoulder. E puts the camera above and behind your right shoulder, so the mirror body is in view beside your head. The mouse still turns your head. The brain and your own body keep their size and place; the mirror body slides away by the 1.4 m the camera moved back and returns when you press E again.
  • Forearms without plates. Both forearms twist smoothly along their length when the palm turns, so nothing sticks out at the elbow, palm up or palm down.

The colours and movements are generated by game rules. They are not recordings of a person, electrical stimulation, decoded intent or a validated model of muscle force. A movement staying until its opposite is switched on is a rule of this game, not something measured, and the glow's timing is a display choice, not physiology. The anatomical surfaces come from open atlases; their presence does not establish a precise functional brain map.

Controls

  • Move the mouse or trackpad to look. It turns your head and neck, then your chest and trunk. Click the game once to capture the pointer; that click does nothing else.
  • Rest your look on a motor area for about a third of a second, or click it: that body part moves as far as it can go and stays there.
  • Click a muscle, on your body or on the mirror body, to switch on the motor area that moves it. A click on a bone does nothing.
  • W/S move the left hand forward and back; A/D move it left and right. You can combine keys. The strip part and the muscles that make the movement light up.
  • Hold Space on the brain, the mirror body or one of your limbs to take hold of it. While Space is held, W moves it farther, S closer, A/D sideways, and your look carries it up and down; the mirror body stays on the ground. Let go of Space to leave it there. Or tap Space once to take hold and keep holding, and tap again to let go.
  • Hold Shift as you click, press Space or drag to reach the other thing on the same line of sight.
  • Press on the brain or the mirror body and drag to turn it; drag the mirror body up or down to slide it away or back. Drag across one of your limbs to turn it like a dial.
  • Click an arrow for a small turn or step; hold it to keep going. Grey arrows turn the brain in place. Green arrows turn the mirror body (left and right) or slide it along the ground (top and bottom). Press Esc twice for a free pointer.
  • Tap Space twice and all four limbs turn once. Tap twice and hold and they keep turning, slowly. Let go to leave every limb where it is.
  • E switches between your own eyes and the view over your shoulder. It does nothing while Space holds something; the status card then says to let go first.
  • R reads the information: the side cards are on at the start, R adds More detail and the colour key, R again hides all of it, and R again brings the cards back.
  • Esc pauses and shows the instructions. Reset body, Reset brain view and the settings (look speed, invert Y, picture quality, the lifted outer muscles and the “Try this” guide) are on that page.
  • What is under the crosshair is named in the “Looking at” card at the bottom left. To also see the name next to the crosshair, tick the box on the Esc page.

Known limitations

  • The input fixes of 11 November (pointer capture across pause and focus changes, cancelling stale input, a queue for input that arrives between frames) are now part of this version, adapted to the new keys. Scripted tests cover their rules; they have not been tried in several browsers.
  • This build has only been run in one desktop browser (Chromium, with scripted mouse and key input and screenshots), by its author and by other instances of the same model that wrote it, which also read the code; what they found was fixed or is listed here. That is not an independent review, which is still to come. Firefox, Safari, touch screens, phones, screen readers and reduced-motion settings are unchecked.
  • The game needs a keyboard and a mouse or trackpad. Window sizes were tried in a desktop browser only. In a window taller than it is wide, the panels cover the lower part of the mirror body; in the view over your shoulder they cover the lower part of both bodies, and the preview badge can sit over the last lines of the panel.
  • Collision checks use spheres that cover the drawn meshes, so items stop a few centimetres apart rather than at their surfaces. Your own body parts are not checked against each other, so one limb can pass through another. A limb's move is checked where it ends in each frame, not along the way. The brain's glass and its inside are kept off the bodies, but the deep cleft between its two halves is not: a brain carried toward you can end with both bodies sitting in that cleft, with glass on both sides. Turning your head is not checked. The checks keep a margin round your left arm's reach, so when the brain or the mirror body rests close to you, a steep look or a trunk bend can stop before anything touches.
  • The crosshair crosses motor areas while you look around. Resting on one for about a third of a second moves that body part, and it stays moved. The shoulder and hip turns of the limb turn (Space tapped twice) and of a limb dial have no motor area of their own, so only another turn, another dial turn or Reset body brings them back. Two held right-shoulder movements in different planes do not add up: the later one brings the other back to its start. A held neck movement adds to your look.
  • Space holds a limb when the crosshair is on one of its muscles or bones, even seen through the brain’s clear glass. Right at the edge of a foot, Space can still take the brain behind it while the view is settling after a quick look. The closed left hand only shows that Space holds something; it does not wrap round what is carried.
  • At the start the mirror body and your own feet sit in front of the leg end of the motor strip. The look still reaches those areas, and the card says what is in front. The toe areas lie on the inner wall between the two halves of the brain and may not face you at the start; turning the brain changes which areas face you. How many of the 54 areas the crosshair can rest on from the start has not been counted again since the brain was made smaller.
  • The lowest face areas, such as the tongue and the throat, lie mostly on the side of the brain. From the start view only their far tips can be aimed at, with the head turned far. A drag or an arrow turns the brain to bring them round, and a line in the status card says so.
  • Some areas and movements have nothing to show. The tongue area has no movement of its own. Your left shoulder already follows your look and the keys; for W, A, S and D the card also names shoulder-blade muscles that this body cannot light. 111 muscles that the earlier anatomy set lacked are now drawn (back, belly, inner thigh, hip, shin, foot and neck), so every movement lights muscles that are there. Over the belly, the sheet of the outer oblique muscle is not drawn where it lies in front of the rectus muscle, as on an atlas plate with the sheath opened; the strip between the two recti is kept. The deep back muscle multifidus is left out because the two atlases do not line up there. Nothing is drawn in place of a missing muscle.
  • The skull is now closed and has eyes, but the top of the head is bare bone: neither atlas has the tendon sheet of the scalp. The muscles at the back of the head are flat sheets in the source, the lips are parted over bone, and there are no muscles at the back of the neck. Where the two atlases' skulls differ, the scalp muscles are lifted onto the bone for display (at most 8.5 mm).
  • The finger and toe bones in the anatomy files end in coarse cones. They are drawn from a smoothed copy with rounded tips (a display change of at most 2.5 mm; the files are unchanged), but at a distance the bare, slightly curled fingers can still read as claws.
  • At strong elbow bends, above about 120 degrees, the inner elbow crease folds in on itself. During the limb turn the left shoulder muscle (deltoid) can show a pointed edge near the camera. When you look far to the left, the left shoulder can come away from its socket; this is not fixed yet.
  • The outlines also outline any faults in the shapes they follow. The gyrus relief of the motor strip is modest at the start view, where the strip is seen mostly from above. Seen through the glass brain, the mirror body looks darker than your own body, and from a distance its belly shows a fine speckle.
  • Only light brighter than white blooms, so the flash on a brain area, which is already lit while you look at it, is fainter than the flash on the muscles. A fast look across the strip lights many areas in a row, each with its own flash. This has not been checked against guidance for people who are sensitive to flashing light. The game is written to drop the flashes when your computer asks for reduced motion, but that has not been tried in a browser.
  • The arrow tips are placed in pairs at the same height or on the same centre line, but they are fixed points in the scene: looking far to one side tilts a pair by a few pixels, and after a limb turn your own hand or feet can hide one tip of a pair. The mirror body's side tips sit above its centre line, where your left hand does not cover them. With the left elbow bent, your left hand can cover a brain arrow you aim at with the crosshair, and that arrow then takes no click; use a free pointer (Esc twice) or a drag instead.
  • Muscle and bone names in the status card are the atlas's own names, for example “Distal phalanx of left thumb”.
  • Near the edges of the view things look stretched, as in any wide-angle picture. A mirror body carried close and to one side looks bent.
  • In the author's scripted runs on one desktop computer at 1920 by 1080 pixels and full picture quality, standing still drew 60 frames a second, looking around 53 to 60, the slow limb turn 52 to 54, holding or carrying the mirror body 46 to 50, and looking around in the view over your shoulder 47 to 55. Some frames still take longer than a thirtieth of a second. Before the 12 November changes the same run drew about 34 frames a second while holding the mirror body, about 43 while carrying it with one stall of about a quarter of a second, and had dropped to the roughest picture by its end. With Picture quality: Auto the game switches to a cheaper picture only when that makes the frames faster. Low always draws simplified meshes, which look rougher.
  • Joint ranges are game values within commonly quoted adult ranges, not measured on this body or on anyone. The left game arm stops short of them in places (finger and wrist), its sideways wrist bends only light their area, and its shoulder blade and collarbone slide with the arm rather than rotate. A lifted thigh stops at 110 degrees, where the rigid thigh and belly shapes would start to pass into each other. Because your heel also stays on the ground, a knee bend from the start goes only part of the way: the knee rises a little instead of folding fully. Joint mechanics, muscle deformation, whole-body coupling and the registration of motor areas are simplified and not validated. Some areas stand for overlapping movement controls rather than established stimulation sites. A visible response is illustrative, not proof that a brain location would cause that movement in a person.
