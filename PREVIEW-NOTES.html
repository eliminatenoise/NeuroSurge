Play NeuroSurge · Source code · Desktop launchers

Make the connection visible.

Reach with W/S or grasp with Space to watch linked brain and muscle colours. Sideways arm inputs do not yet have matching muscle glow. NeuroSurge turns that connection into a browser game: you can inspect a skinless body, grasp, turn the palm, move the brain overview and explore its movement controls. Your computer runs the 3D scene and simulation; the host supplies the files.

What this preview shows

The wider-view and object-carry preview, published 10 November 2026, opens up more of the scene and makes the mirror body larger in view. You can carry the brain or mirror body with the same controls, then leave it where you release it.

The colours and movements are generated by game rules. They are not recordings of a person, electrical stimulation, decoded intent or a validated model of muscle force. The anatomical surfaces come from open atlases; their presence does not establish a precise functional brain map.

Controls

  • Move the trackpad or mouse to look. Click the game to capture the pointer.
  • W/S move the left arm forward/back; A/D move it left/right. You can combine keys.
  • Hold Space to close all five digits; release to open. While looking at the brain or visible mirror body, hold Space to carry it. Move the mouse or trackpad to reposition it; W moves it farther away, S brings it closer, and A/D move it sideways. Release Space to leave it there. Move the brain aside before trying to grasp the mirror body behind it.
  • Double-tap Space to turn the palm. E switches between first and third person.
  • Look at a movement region to activate it briefly. Click again to repeat. Other body parts respond through their brain controls.
  • Click or hold a compass arrow to adjust the brain or mirror-body view.
  • Esc opens the instructions and pauses. Reset Body is on that page. Esc resumes; the browser may require another click to capture the pointer.

Known limitations

  • The hand is too low in third-person view. That positioning repair is still being tested.
  • Some sideways arm inputs lack matching muscle glow. Joint mechanics, muscle deformation, whole-body coupling, precise motor-region registration and all control/view combinations remain incompletely validated.
  • The mirror appears larger, but its world position still needs to come closer. Near/far movement limits and regrasping need further browser checks.
  • Some regions represent overlapping movement controls, rather than individually established stimulation sites. A visible response is illustrative, not proof that looking at that brain location would cause that movement in a person.
  • Timing, performance and anatomy visibility vary with the computer and viewport. The complete original acceptance checks have not all passed.
